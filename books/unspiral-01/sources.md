# Sources: Unspiral

Every factual claim in the manuscript maps to an entry here. Verified = the source exists and
supports the claim as worded in the book. Re-read each original before publishing.

| # | Claim as used in the book | Where used | Source | Verified |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | Repetitive thought can be constructive (planning, problem-solving, recovery) or unconstructive (linked to anxiety and low mood); concrete "how" processing tends to be constructive, abstract "why" processing unconstructive. | Intro, Ch 2 | Watkins, E. R. (2008). Constructive and unconstructive repetitive thought. *Psychological Bulletin*, 134(2), 163–206. | Yes (2026-10-05, abstract) |
| S2 | Rumination enhances negative thinking and impairs problem solving; in classic experiments, people in a low mood who did a short distracting task felt better than people asked to ruminate. | Ch 2, Ch 3 | Nolen-Hoeksema, S., Wisco, B. E., & Lyubomirsky, S. (2008). Rethinking rumination. *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 3(5), 400–424. | Yes (2026-10-05, summary of the review) |
| S3 | In a randomized study (46 people), two weeks of worrying only during a fixed 30-minute time-and-place window each day reduced worry, anxiety and insomnia symptoms more than a comparison condition. | Ch 3 | McGowan, S. K., & Behar, E. (2013). A preliminary investigation of stimulus control training for worry: Effects on anxiety and insomnia. *Behavior Modification*, 37(1), 90–112 (volume/pages to confirm). | Yes (2026-10-05, abstract) |
| S4 | People told not to think of a white bear couldn't stop, and afterward thought about it more than people who'd been allowed to think about it from the start. | Ch 2 | Wegner, D. M., Schneider, D. J., Carter, S. R., & White, T. L. (1987). Paradoxical effects of thought suppression. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 53(1), 5–13. | Yes (2026-10-05) |
| S5 | In adults, putting a feeling into words reduced activity in the amygdala, a brain area that reacts strongly to threats. | Ch 3 | Lieberman, M. D., et al. (2007). Putting feelings into words. *Psychological Science*, 18(5), 421–428. | Yes (Calm Words Book 1, 2026-09-30) |
| S6 | A defusion exercise (repeating a word out loud quickly) reduced how believable and how upsetting negative self-thoughts felt, more than distraction or thought control did. | Ch 3 | Masuda, A., Hayes, S. | S7 | Young adults who spent 5 minutes before bed writing a specific to-do list fell asleep faster (about 15 vs 25 minutes) than those who wrote about tasks already done. | Ch 3 (and Ch 8) | Scullin, M. The effects of bedtime writing on difficulty falling asleep. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 147(1), 139–146 (volume/pages to confirm). | Yes (2026-10-05, Baylor news release) |
| S8 | People who always seek the best option ("maximizers") reported more regret and less satisfaction than people who choose "good enough" ("satisficers"). | Ch 3 (and Ch 7) | Schwartz, B., et al. (2002). Maximizing versus satisficing: Happiness is a matter of choice. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 83(5), 1178–1197. | Yes (2026-10-05, abstract) |
| S9 | The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US) offers free, confidential, 24/7 call, text and chat support for any emotional distress. | Front matter, Ch 10 | 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, 988lifeline.org | Yes (2026-10-05) |

Not sourced on purpose (presented as practical tools, not research findings): the 2-Minute
Unspiral, Solve or Park, the three spiral types (Replay, What-If, Can't-Decide),
Best–Worst–Likely, the Tool Chooser, Change the Channel as a name (the research behind it is S2).

Dropped: Pennebaker (1997) expressive writing. It studies multi-day writing about emotional
or traumatic events, not a 2-minute brain dump, so it doesn't support the Brain Dump claim.
