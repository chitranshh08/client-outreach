# Chapter 2: Why Your Mind Loops

Tom is 41. Three weeks ago, he got a job offer. Better pay, longer commute, a smaller team.

Since then he has read every review of the company he could find. He has built a
spreadsheet with nineteen rows. He has asked his partner, his brother, two old colleagues and
a friend from college what they would do. He has lain awake running both futures forward,
year by year.

He still doesn't know. And the deadline for answering is Friday.

If you asked Tom what he's been doing for three weeks, he'd say "thinking it through." It
certainly feels that way. But somewhere in week one, the thinking stopped adding anything
new. Since then, he's been walking the same loop, a little more tired each time.

This chapter is about why that happens, and why the most common advice ("just stop thinking
about it") makes it worse.

## The loop feels like help

Here's the tricky part about overthinking: it feels useful. Going over a conversation feels
like learning from it. Running what-ifs feels like getting ready. Researching one more option
feels like being careful.

And sometimes it is. Thinking things over can help people prepare, plan and recover from
hard experiences. Researchers who study repetitive thinking call that the constructive kind.
The same research describes an unconstructive kind, linked to more anxiety and lower mood,
that circles without arriving anywhere.[^S1]

So the question is never "Am I thinking too much?" It's "Is this thinking going anywhere?"

## How to tell the two apart

Here's a simple test. Constructive thinking **ends in something**: a plan, a decision, a
lesson, an action. Overthinking **ends where it started**, just more tired.

**Thinking that helps**
- asks "What's the next step?"
- gets more specific over time
- ends in a plan or a decision
- feels like work, then relief

**Thinking that loops**
- asks "Why did this happen to me?"
- stays vague, or gets bigger and scarier
- ends where it started
- feels like work, then more worry

The first line matters most. Research on repetitive thought suggests that **concrete "how"
thinking** tends to be constructive, while **abstract "why" thinking** tends to keep people
stuck.[^S1] "Why am I like this?" has no answer you can act on. "What's one thing I can do
before Friday?" does.

## What looping does to you

Going around the same loop doesn't just waste time. Researchers who review studies on
rumination (the habit of dwelling on problems and their causes) have found that it tends to
make negative thinking stronger and problem-solving harder.[^S2]

That explains a lot of Tom's three weeks. The longer he looped, the scarier both options
looked, and the less able he felt to choose. More thinking didn't bring clarity. It brought
fog.

## Why "just stop thinking about it" doesn't work

If looping is the problem, why not just stop?

Because trying not to think about something tends to backfire. In a classic experiment,
psychologist Daniel Wegner asked people not to think about a white bear for five minutes.
They couldn't do it; the bear kept popping up. And afterward, when they were allowed to think
about anything, they thought about the white bear more than people who had been told to think
about it from the start.[^S4]

Pushing a thought away seems to keep it close. That's why this book never asks you to stop
thinking. Instead, it gives the thought somewhere to go: an action, a scheduled time, a
label, or a different task. You're not fighting the loop. You're showing it the exit.

## What feeds the loop

Some conditions make spirals more likely. Knowing them helps you see a loop coming.

- **Being tired.** Loops tend to be louder at the end of a long day, and loudest in bed.
- **Empty time.** The commute, the shower, the wait in a queue: unfilled minutes are where
  loops start.
- **One more search.** Looking up another review, another symptom, another opinion feels like
  progress. It often adds fuel instead of answers.
- **Reassurance on repeat.** Asking someone "Do you think it's okay?" can bring relief for a
  few minutes, and then the question comes back, needing to be asked again.
- **"Why" questions.** As the research above suggests, "why" keeps the loop going. "What
  next" moves it forward.

None of these are bad things on their own. They're just the places to watch.

## The three spirals, and what each one wants

Every spiral is your mind trying to protect you, in a clumsy way. Knowing what each one is
trying to do tells you what it needs.

**The Replay** is trying to make sure you learn from the past, so it doesn't happen again.
What it needs: **one lesson, then permission to let go.** "Next time, I'll pause before I
answer. That's the lesson. Done."

**The What-If** is trying to keep you safe from something that might happen. What it needs:
**a plan for the likely outcome, and a place to park the rest.** You can prepare for what's
likely. You can't prepare for everything that's possible.

**The Can't-Decide** is trying to protect you from regret. What it needs: **a deadline and a
"good enough" standard.** Waiting for the perfect choice usually means making no choice, and
that's a choice too.

You'll see these three needs again and again in Part 2. Every tool in the next chapter gives
one of them what it's looking for.

## One Small Step

**Catch and name three spirals today.** Don't fix anything. Just notice when you're in a loop
and name it: Replay, What-If or Can't-Decide. Add each one to your tally from Chapter 1.

By the end of the day, you may see a pattern. Many people find that one spiral shows up
more than the others. That's the one we'll work on first.
