# Chapter 10: When It Won't Stop

Three weeks after the meeting where she said "That won't work," Rachel from Chapter 1 still
spirals. Last Tuesday she replayed a phone call for most of an evening. On Friday she lay
awake with a What-If about her mother's health.

But something has changed. When she looks at the tally in her phone, she can see it. In the
first week, most spirals ran for hours. Now most of them are over in ten or twenty minutes.
She catches them sooner. She knows which tool to reach for. And the Replay about the
meeting? It hasn't come back in days.

Rachel hasn't stopped overthinking. She's gotten faster at stopping it. That's what
progress looks like.

This chapter is for the times when it doesn't feel that way: when a loop keeps coming
back, when the tools seem to stop working, or when worry feels like more than a habit.

## Measure the right thing

If you judge yourself by "Did I overthink today?", the answer will often be yes, and you'll
feel like you're failing. Measure these instead:

- **How long** did the spiral last?
- **How soon** did you notice it?
- **Did you find a way out,** even if it came back later?

Your tally from Chapter 1 helps here. Once a week, look at it and ask: *Am I catching them
sooner? Are they shorter?* Small changes count. Going from three hours to forty minutes is
a big win, even if the spiral still happened.

## Six common snags, and what to do

**1. The Worry Window has turned into a worry day.**
If you're running the window at 5:30, then again at 8, then in bed, it's not a window
anymore. Go back to the rule: one set time, fifteen to thirty minutes, not in bed. Between
windows, the only job is writing the worry down and saying **"Not now."**

**2. The Brain Dump has turned into a diary.**
If your two-minute list has become three pages on why everything is going wrong, the loop
has found its way in. Set the timer again, and keep to bullet points. Tasks and worries,
not explanations.

**3. You keep asking for reassurance.**
"Do you think it's okay?" can bring relief for a few minutes, and then the question comes
back. If you notice you're asking the same person the same question again, that's the loop
asking. Answer it yourself, once, with the line from the card. Then change the channel.

**4. You're parking things that need solving.**
Parking is for things you can't act on. If the same worry keeps coming back to the Worry
Window day after day, ask again: *Is there really nothing I can do?* Often there's a small
step you've been avoiding: a call, a conversation, an appointment. Take it, and the worry
usually gets quieter.

**5. The tools have become one more thing to do perfectly.**
You don't need the perfect tool or the perfect words. If you've spent ten minutes choosing
which tool to use, that's a Can't-Decide. Go back to the basics: notice, name it, solve or
park, one small step.

**6. Checking has taken over.**
Checking the chat, the forecast, your bank balance, your body. Each check brings a moment of
relief and keeps the loop alive. Pick one thing you check too often and give it a set
time, once or twice a day.

## When to talk to a professional

The tools in this book are for everyday overthinking. Sometimes worry is more than a
habit, and that's not a personal failing. It's a sign that you deserve more support than a
book can give.

Please talk to a doctor or a mental health professional if any of these sound like you:

- **Worry is affecting your daily life,** and it feels hard to control, even when you use
  the tools.[^S15]
- **You worry most days, about lots of different things,** and it's been going on for a long
  time (months, not weeks).[^S15]
- **Your body is joining in a lot:** trouble sleeping, feeling restless or tense, getting
  tired easily, trouble concentrating, stomach problems, a racing heart or feeling
  dizzy.[^S15]
- **You've had trouble sleeping for months,** or poor sleep is making it hard to cope with
  daily life.[^S16]
- **You have panic attacks:** sudden waves of fear with a pounding heart, shaking or
  trouble breathing.
- **Your mood is low** most of the time, or you've lost interest in things you used to
  enjoy.
- **You have thoughts that frighten you** and won't leave, or you feel you have to do
  certain things over and over to feel okay.
- **You're relying on alcohol, drugs or anything else** to quiet your mind.
- **It's affecting your work or your relationships.**

**Where to start:** your doctor is a good first step. They can talk it through with you,
check for anything physical, and point you toward support such as talking therapy. In
England, adults can also refer themselves to NHS Talking Therapies without seeing a doctor
first.[^S15] In other countries, your doctor or a local mental health service can tell you
what's available.

**If you're in crisis or thinking about harming yourself,** please reach out now. In the US,
you can call or text **988** (the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), any time, day or
night.[^S9] In other countries, call your local emergency number or crisis line. If you're in
immediate danger, call emergency services.

Getting help isn't the opposite of using this book. Many people use both. The tools you've
learned here can sit right alongside the help a professional gives you.

## One Small Step

**Look back at your tally, and write down the one tool that's helped you most so far.** Not
the one you think should work best. The one that actually got you out of a loop. That's
your first tool from now on, and Part 3 will build on it.

If anything in the list above sounded like you, make the other small step today: **book an
appointment with your doctor.**
