# Chapter 3: Your Eight Tools (and Which One to Use When)

The 2-Minute Unspiral gets you out of a loop. This chapter gives you the tools to use inside
step 3, "solve or park," and in the moments when a loop is stubborn.

There are only eight. That's on purpose. A list of thirty techniques is just one more thing
to overthink. Each of these eight is matched to the spiral it works best on, and at the end
of the chapter there's a one-page chooser, so you never have to wonder which one to pick.

---

## Tool 1: The Worry Window

**Best for:** What-Ifs, and anything you parked.

**What it is:** a short, fixed time each day when you're allowed to worry on purpose. The rest
of the day, when a worry shows up, you write it down and save it for the window.

**How to do it:**

1. **Pick a time and a place.** Many people pick late afternoon. Somewhere that
   isn't your bed. Fifteen to thirty minutes.
2. **During the day, park worries.** When one shows up, write it in a note on your phone or a
   small notebook, and say to yourself: **"Not now. That's for 5:30."**
3. **In the window, worry properly.** Go through the list. For each item, ask the solve-or-park
   question again. Some of them may have shrunk. Some will turn into actions.
4. **When the time is up, stop.** Close the notebook. Anything left waits for tomorrow's window.

**Why it works:** it gives your worry a time and a place instead of letting it run all day. In
one study, people who restricted their worrying to a 30-minute window in the same place each
day for two weeks reported less worry, less anxiety and fewer sleep problems than people in a
comparison group.[^S3]

## Tool 2: Solve or Park

**Best for:** every spiral. It's step 3 of the 2-Minute Unspiral.

**How to do it:** ask, **"Is there one thing I can do about this in the next 24 hours?"** If
yes, do it or schedule it now. If no, park it in the Worry Window.

**Tip:** keep the action tiny. "Fix my career" isn't an action. "Email Sam to ask for
feedback" is.

---

## Tool 3: Name It

**Best for:** every spiral, especially when the feeling is strong.

**How to do it:** in a few words, name the spiral and the feeling.

**"This is the Replay. I feel embarrassed."**
**"What-If again. I'm scared."**

**Why it works:** naming what's happening creates a little distance from it. There's research
behind the feeling part: in one brain-imaging study, adults who put their feelings into words
showed less activity in the amygdala, a part of the brain that reacts strongly to threats.[^S5]
That study was about labeling feelings, not thoughts, so treat it as a clue, not proof. But many
people find that a loop with a name feels smaller.

---

## Tool 4: The Thought Label

**Best for:** Replays, and harsh thoughts about yourself ("I'm so stupid," "I always ruin
things").

**What it is:** instead of arguing with a thought, you step back and label it as a thought.
Psychologists call this **cognitive defusion**.

**How to do it:** put this phrase in front of the thought:

**"I'm having the thought that…"**

"I'm an idiot" becomes **"I'm having the thought that I'm an idiot."** Then, if you like, one
step further: **"I notice I'm having the thought that I'm an idiot."**

Nothing about the thought has changed, but now you're looking at it rather than through it.

**A strange but useful variation:** say the hardest word in the thought out loud, quickly,
over and over for about 30 seconds: "stupid, stupid, stupid…" After a while it turns into a
sound. In one study, this exact exercise made negative thoughts about oneself feel less
believable and less upsetting than either distraction or trying to control the thought.[^S6]
(Best done somewhere private.)

---

## Tool 5: The Brain Dump

**Best for:** busy, crowded loops, and especially loops at night.

**How to do it:**

1. Set a timer for two to five minutes.
2. Write down everything on your mind: tasks, worries, half-thoughts. No sorting, no fixing.
3. At night, make it a **specific to-do list for tomorrow and the next few days**.
4. When the timer goes off, close the notebook. Don't reread it until morning.

**Why it works:** your mind doesn't have to keep holding it all. In one sleep-lab study, young
adults who spent five minutes before bed writing a specific to-do list fell asleep faster than
people who wrote about tasks they had already finished (about 15 minutes versus 25). The more
specific the list, the faster they fell asleep.[^S7]

**Watch out:** a brain dump is a list, not a diary. If you notice yourself writing pages about
why something happened, stop and go back to bullet points.

---

## Tool 6: Best–Worst–Likely

**Best for:** What-Ifs.

**How to do it:** take the thing you're worried about and write three short lines:

- **Best case:** what happens if it goes well?
- **Worst case:** what happens if it goes badly? (Write it down. It's often less frightening
  on paper than in your head.)
- **Most likely:** honestly, what will probably happen?

Then make a plan for the **most likely** case only, and one first step for the worst case
("If that happens, I'll call Mia"). Park the rest.

**Say this to yourself:** **"I can plan for likely. I can't plan for everything."**

---

## Tool 7: The Good-Enough Rule

**Best for:** Can't-Decide.

**What it is:** instead of hunting for the best possible option, you decide in advance what
"good enough" looks like, then take the first option that meets it.

**How to do it:**

1. **Write your must-haves.** Three, at most. ("Under $500. Fits the corner. Washable.")
2. **Set a deadline.** A real one, with a time: "by Friday at noon."
3. **Pick the first option that meets all the must-haves.** Then stop looking.

**Why it works:** the search for the perfect choice has a cost. Researchers who compared
"maximizers" (people who always look for the best option) with "satisficers" (people happy
with good enough) found that maximizers reported more regret and less satisfaction with their
choices.[^S8]

**Say this to yourself:** **"Good enough, chosen, beats perfect, still searching."**

---

## Tool 8: Change the Channel

**Best for:** Replays and What-Ifs that keep coming back right now, when you can't do anything
about them.

**How to do it:** give your mind something absorbing for ten to twenty minutes. A walk with a
podcast. A puzzle. Cooking something with steps. A call with a friend about something else.

**Why it works:** in classic experiments with people in a low mood, those who spent a few
minutes on a distracting task felt better than those who were asked to dwell on their
feelings.[^S2] Changing the channel isn't avoiding the problem forever. It's getting out of the
loop now, so you can come back to the problem later, in the Worry Window, with a clearer head.

**Watch out:** endless scrolling isn't changing the channel. It can feed the loop instead.
Pick something with a beginning and an end.

---

## The Tool Chooser

When you're stuck, find your spiral below and start with the first tool. If it doesn't help
after a few minutes, try the backup.

**The Replay** (the past)
- First: **Name It**, then the **Thought Label**
- Backup: **Change the Channel**
- Then: one lesson, and park it

**The What-If** (the future)
- First: **Best–Worst–Likely**
- Backup: park it in the **Worry Window**

**The Can't-Decide** (a choice)
- First: the **Good-Enough Rule**
- Backup: **Solve or Park** (the action is "decide by…")

**At night** (any spiral)
- First: the **Brain Dump**
- Backup: **"This goes in tomorrow's Worry Window"**

**Always:** the 2-Minute Unspiral first. Notice, name it, solve or park, one small step.

## Tom's decision

Remember Tom, with the job offer and the nineteen-row spreadsheet? Here's how his week ended.

On Tuesday night, lying awake, he did a brain dump instead of running both futures again. On
Wednesday, he used the Good-Enough Rule. His must-haves: pay that covers the commute, a
manager he trusts, and getting home before 6:30 at least three days a week. Deadline: Thursday
at 8 p.m.

On Thursday, he called the hiring manager with two questions about working hours. The answers
met all three must-haves. He accepted at 7:40 p.m.

Did he still wonder about the other path? A little, once or twice. When he did, he named it
(**"Can't-Decide, after the fact"**), reminded himself that the choice met every must-have, and
changed the channel. The loop that had run for three weeks was done in three days.

## One Small Step

**Set up tomorrow's Worry Window.** Pick a time (15 to 30 minutes) and a place that isn't your
bed. Put it in your calendar now. Then, for the rest of today, when a worry shows up, write it
down and say, **"Not now. That's for the window."**
