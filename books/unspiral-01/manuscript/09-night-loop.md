# Chapter 8: The Night Loop

Nadia is 45. She's tired all day. She yawns through the afternoon and promises herself an
early night. At 10:30 she turns off the light.

At 10:31, her brain turns on.

The dentist appointment she forgot to book. The email she didn't answer. Whether her son is
okay at his new school. A comment her sister made at a family lunch, months
ago. Something she has to remember to buy. Something she has to remember to say.
By midnight she's checked the time four times, and each time, the math gets worse: *If I
fall asleep now, I'll get six hours. Five and a half. Five.*

Night is when every spiral gets louder. It's quiet, it's dark, there's nothing else for your
mind to hold, and you're too tired to argue with it. A worry that would take thirty seconds
at 2 p.m. can take two hours at 2 a.m.

The cards in this chapter use two tools above all: the **Brain Dump**, to get things out of
your head and onto paper, and the **Worry Window**, so your mind knows there's a time for
this, and it isn't now. Before you start, put a notebook and a pen by your bed. You'll need
them.

---

## Moment 23: Can't fall asleep because your brain won't stop

**The spiral:** Any of the three. At night they tend to come all at once.

**What's going on:** The day's unfinished business floods in as soon as there's nothing
else to think about. Your mind is trying not to forget things. Ironically, the effort of
holding them keeps you awake.

**Try this:** The Brain Dump, bedtime version.

1. Before you turn off the light, sit up and write for five minutes.
2. Make it a **specific to-do list** for tomorrow and the next few days. "Call the dentist
   at 9." "Reply to Jo about Friday." Not a diary. A list.
3. Close the notebook. Put it on the floor or in a drawer.

There's good evidence for this one. In the sleep-lab study from Chapter 3, people who wrote
a specific to-do list before bed fell asleep faster than people who wrote about tasks
they'd already finished.[^S7]

**Say this to yourself:**
**"It's on the list. I don't have to hold it."**

**If it keeps going:** If you've been lying awake for around 20 minutes, or you've started to
worry about not sleeping, NHS sleep advice suggests getting up. Go to another room, keep
the lights dim, and do something quiet and calm, like reading a paper book, until you feel
sleepy. Then go back to bed.[^S13] Lying in bed fighting it tends to teach your brain that
bed is where you worry.

**One small step:** Put a notebook and pen by your bed tonight.

---

## Moment 24: Waking at 3 a.m. and starting the list

**The spiral:** What-If, usually. At 3 a.m., everything looks like a disaster.

**What's going on:** You wake up, and before you've even checked the time, your mind is
running. Things that felt manageable during the day now feel huge. That's the hour, not the
problem. Tired, half-awake minds are very bad judges of how serious things are.

**Try this:** Park it, then Change the Channel, quietly.

1. Notice and name it: **"I'm spiraling. It's 3 a.m. Everything looks worse at 3 a.m."**
2. If something new is bothering you, write one line in the notebook by your bed. Don't
   read it. Don't solve it. **"That's for tomorrow's Worry Window."**
3. Give your mind something gentle and dull to hold. Count your breaths out to ten, then
   start again. Picture the route of a walk you know well, step by step. Something
   absorbing enough to hold your attention, but not exciting.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"Nothing gets solved at 3 a.m. It'll be smaller in the morning."**

**If it keeps going:** Don't check your phone. The light wakes you more, and one glance at
email or the news can start a fresh loop. If you're wide awake after about 20 minutes, get
up and do something quiet in dim light until you feel sleepy, as in Moment 23.[^S13]

**One small step:** Turn the clock away from you, so you're not doing sleep math every
time you wake.

---

## Moment 25: Replaying the day in bed

**The spiral:** Replay.

**What's going on:** Bed is the first quiet moment of the day, so the Replay rushes in: what
you said in the meeting, what you should have said to your partner, the thing you forgot.
Tiredness makes the review harsh.

**Try this:** A mini Worry Window, before bed.

1. **Ten minutes before bed, not in bed,** go over the day once, on purpose. Was there one
   lesson? One thing to do tomorrow? Write it down.
2. Then close the day with one line: **"Today's done. Anything left goes in tomorrow's
   window."**
3. In bed, if a replay starts, you already have your answer. **"I went over that. It's
   handled."**

This is the Worry Window in miniature: a short, set time for going over things, so your
mind doesn't save it all for bed.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"I already reviewed today. Bed is for sleeping."**

**If it keeps going:** Use the Thought Label on the line that stings: **"I'm having the
thought that I made a fool of myself."** Then go back to your breath count, or your walk
route.

**One small step:** Set a reminder for ten minutes before your bedtime: "Close the day."

---

## One Small Step

**Set up your night kit tonight.** A notebook and pen by the bed. The clock turned away. Your
phone charging in another room, or at least out of reach. And a ten-minute "close the day"
reminder before bed. It takes five minutes to set up, and you'll be glad of it at 3 a.m.

---

**POCKET CARD: WHEN YOU'RE AWAKE AT NIGHT**

1. Notice: "I'm spiraling. It's night. Everything looks worse at night."
2. New worry? One line in the notebook. "That's for tomorrow's window."
3. Don't solve. Don't check your phone.
4. Count breaths to ten, or walk a familiar route in your head.
5. Awake for about 20 minutes? Get up, dim light, something quiet. Back to bed when sleepy.
