# Part 1: How to Unspiral

*Read this part first. It takes about 20 minutes, and you can use it on the next loop.*

# Chapter 1: The 2-Minute Unspiral

Rachel is 34. In this morning's team meeting, her manager suggested a new plan, and Rachel
said, "That won't work." Out loud. In front of everyone.

The meeting moved on. Nobody seemed to mind. But on the drive home, Rachel replays it. She
hears her own voice, sharper than she meant. She pictures her manager's face. By dinner she
has rewritten the moment six different ways. By bedtime she's wondering whether this is the
thing that ends her chances of a promotion.

Nothing new has happened since 10 a.m. Rachel has just been going around the same loop for
eleven hours.

Now rewind. On the drive home, Rachel catches herself on the third replay. She thinks,
*Okay. I'm spiraling.* She names it: *This is a replay, and I'm embarrassed.* She asks
herself one question: *Is there one thing I can do about this in the next 24 hours?* There
is. She can send her manager a short note tomorrow: "I was blunt about the new plan this
morning. Here's what I was worried about." At the next red light, she adds a reminder to
her phone. Then she turns on a podcast.

The thought still comes back once or twice that evening. But now it has somewhere to go:
tomorrow's note. And each time, the loop is shorter.

Same meeting. Same comment. Same Rachel. The difference was four small moves, and they took
about two minutes.

## The 2-Minute Unspiral

The **2-Minute Unspiral** is a short sequence that works on almost any loop. It has four
steps.

**1. Notice.**
**2. Name it.**
**3. Solve or park.**
**4. Take one small step.**

Let's take them one at a time.

## Step 1: Notice

You can't get out of a loop until you know you're in one. And loops are sneaky. They feel
like thinking, so they don't announce themselves.

Here are some signs you've slipped from thinking into spiraling:

- You've had the **same thought three times** without getting anywhere new.
- You're asking questions that have **no answer**: "Why did I say that?" "What if
  everything goes wrong?"
- **Time has vanished.** You picked up your phone to check one thing, and it's been forty
  minutes.
- Your **body has joined in**: tight jaw, shallow breathing, a knot in your stomach.

When you spot one of these, say it to yourself, plainly:

**"I'm spiraling."**

That's all. No judgment, no "why am I like this?" Just noticing. That one sentence turns you
from someone inside the loop into someone watching it.

## Step 2: Name It

Most overthinking falls into one of three kinds. Naming which one you're in tells you what
to do next.

**The Replay** is about the past. A conversation, a mistake, an awkward moment you keep
going over. *"Why did I say that?"*

**The What-If** is about the future. Something that might go wrong. *"What if they say no?
What if I get sick? What if it's a disaster?"*

**The Can't-Decide** is about a choice. You go back and forth, gather more information, ask
more people, and still can't pick. *"But what if the other one is better?"*

Name the spiral, and the feeling that comes with it, in a few words:

**"This is a Replay, and I'm embarrassed."**
**"This is a What-If. I'm scared."**
**"Can't-Decide again. I'm afraid of choosing wrong."**

Chapter 3 explains why naming helps. For now, just know that a loop with a name is easier to
step out of than one without.

## Step 3: Solve or Park

This is the step that does the heavy lifting. Ask yourself one question:

**"Is there one thing I can do about this in the next 24 hours?"**

**If yes, solve.** Not the whole problem, just the next action. Then do it now, or put it on
your calendar. Rachel's next action was a two-line note to her manager. For a What-If about a
presentation, it might be "practice the opening twice." For a Can't-Decide, it might be
"pick by Friday at noon."

**If no, park it.** Some loops have no action in them. You can't un-say a sentence at a
party. You can't control whether it rains on Saturday. For these, write the thought down in
one line and tell yourself:

**"This goes in tomorrow's Worry Window."**

The Worry Window is a short, scheduled time each day for worrying on purpose. It sounds
strange, but there's research behind it. Chapter 3 shows you how to set one up. Until
then, writing the thought down and choosing a time to come back to it is enough.

### Why "solve or park" works

Researchers who study repetitive thinking have found an important difference. Thinking
about the concrete **how** of a problem ("What's one thing I can do?") tends to help.
Thinking about the abstract **why** ("Why does this always happen to me?") tends to keep
people stuck.[^S1]

"Solve or park" forces the switch. It turns a "why" loop into a "what next" question, and
gives every thought a place to go: an action, or a time.

## Step 4: Take One Small Step

The last step gets your body out of the loop too. Do one small, physical thing right away:

- Send the message, or write the note.
- Put the reminder in your calendar.
- Stand up, get a glass of water, step outside for a minute.
- Start the next task on your list, even just the first two minutes of it.

A small step tells your brain the loop is closed for now. It also gives your attention
something new to hold, which makes the next replay weaker.

## The 2-Minute Unspiral in action

*A What-If before a trip:*
Notice: **"I'm spiraling."** Name it: **"What-If. Worried about missing the flight."**
Solve or park: one thing in 24 hours? Yes. Check in online and set two alarms. Small step: do
it now, then put the phone down.

*A Can't-Decide about a weekend invitation:*
Notice: **"I'm spiraling."** Name it: **"Can't-Decide. I don't want to let her down."**
Solve or park: one thing in 24 hours? Yes. Decide by 6 p.m. tonight. Small step: set a
reminder for 6 p.m. and go back to work.

*A Replay you can't fix:*
Notice: **"I'm spiraling."** Name it: **"Replay. I said something awkward at the party."**
Solve or park: one thing in 24 hours? No. Park it. Write one line for tomorrow's Worry
Window. Small step: put on music and start the dishes.

## Five swaps to try this week

*Instead of:* "Why do I always do this?"
*Try:* **"What's one thing I can do now?"**

*Instead of:* "Stop thinking about it!"
*Try:* **"That's the Replay again."**

*Instead of:* "I need to figure this out tonight."
*Try:* **"This goes in tomorrow's Worry Window."**

*Instead of:* "I need more information first."
*Try:* **"What would I pick if I had to decide in ten minutes?"**

*Instead of:* "What if it all goes wrong?"
*Try:* **"If it goes wrong, what's my first step?"**

## "But it came back!"

It might. A loop you've stepped out of can come back an hour later, or at bedtime. That
isn't failure. Run the 2-Minute Unspiral again. It's often faster the second time, because
the thought already has an answer: *I've got a note for tomorrow* or *That's parked.*

Each time you do this, you're teaching your mind that the loop has an exit. That's how
spirals get shorter: not all at once, but one exit at a time.

## One Small Step

1. **Use the 2-Minute Unspiral on the next three spirals you notice.** Notice, name it,
   solve or park, one small step.
2. **Keep a tally.** Each time you catch a spiral, make a mark in your phone's notes. You'll
   use the count later.

That's it. You now have a way out of any loop.
