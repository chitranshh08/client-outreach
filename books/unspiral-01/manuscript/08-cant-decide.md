# Chapter 7: Can't-Decide

Lena is 31. She needs a new phone, because the old one turns itself off at 30 percent.

That was three weeks ago. Since then, Lena has watched nine review videos, compared four
models in a spreadsheet, read a forum thread about battery life that was 212 replies long,
and put two different phones in her online basket and taken them out again. Her old phone
is now turning itself off at 40 percent.

Lena makes good decisions at work every day. It's not that she can't choose. It's that
every time she gets close, a voice asks, *But what if the other one is better?* And the
search starts again.

That's the Can't-Decide. It's trying to protect you from regret. But it has a blind spot:
**not deciding is also a decision**, usually the worst one available, because it costs you
time and peace while the choice waits.

Remember what the Can't-Decide wants: **a deadline and a "good enough" standard.** Every
card in this chapter gives it those two things.

---

## Moment 18: Small choices that eat an hour

*The menu, the gift, the paint color, the phone*

**The spiral:** Can't-Decide.

**What's going on:** Small choices feel like they should be easy, which makes it more
frustrating when they're not. Online shopping makes it worse: there's always one more
option, one more review, one more comparison.

**Try this:** The Good-Enough Rule, small version.

1. **Two or three must-haves, no more.** "Under $300. Good battery. Fits my hand."
2. **A short deadline.** For small things, minutes, not days: "I'll pick by the end of this
   coffee."
3. **First one that fits wins.** Stop looking as soon as one option meets every must-have.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"Good enough, chosen, beats perfect, still searching."**

**If it keeps going:** Use the ten-minute question from Chapter 1: **"What would I pick if I
had to decide in ten minutes?"** You usually already know. The rest of the search is
looking for permission.

**One small step:** Set a timer for the deadline you chose, and decide when it rings.

---

## Moment 19: Saying yes or no to plans

*The party invitation, the weekend trip, the favor someone asked for*

**The spiral:** Can't-Decide, often mixed with a What-If about how they'll react.

**What's going on:** You're weighing what you want against what you think you should do,
and how the other person will feel. You go back and forth, and the longer you wait, the
harder it gets to answer at all.

**Try this:** Solve or Park, with a deadline and a gut check.

1. **Set a time to answer.** "I'll reply by 6 p.m." Put it in your calendar.
2. **Ask one question:** *If I said yes, would I feel relief or dread when the day comes?*
   Your first reaction is useful information.
3. **Answer by the deadline,** in one or two lines.
   - Yes: **"I'd love to. See you Saturday."**
   - No: **"Thanks so much for asking. I can't make this one, but I'd love to see you
     soon."**

A short, kind no doesn't need a long explanation.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"A clear answer is kinder than a late maybe."**

**If it keeps going:** If you said no and the guilt starts, that's a Replay now, not a
Can't-Decide. Name it: **"Replay. I feel guilty."** Then remind yourself that you answered
kindly and on time.

**One small step:** Put the deadline in your calendar now.

*Saying no without the guilt spiral is the subject of a later book in this series:*
Unspiral the People-Pleaser.

## Moment 20: A big decision

*A job, a move, a relationship step, a big purchase*

**The spiral:** Can't-Decide.

**What's going on:** Big decisions deserve real thought, and some research and advice
really help. The trouble starts when you've stopped learning anything new but keep
going anyway, like Tom and his nineteen-row spreadsheet in Chapter 2.

**Try this:** The Good-Enough Rule, big version.

1. **Gather facts on purpose,** for a set time. "I'll research until Sunday." Write down the
   questions that would actually change your mind, and get answers to those.
2. **Write your must-haves.** Three, at most. The things that matter most to you, not what
   would look best to others.
3. **Set a real deadline,** with a date and time. If there's an outside deadline, set yours
   a day earlier.
4. **Choose the option that meets your must-haves.** If two options both do, pick either.
   They're both good enough.

It may also help to know how regret tends to work. Research on regret suggests that people
regret their actions more in the short term, but in the long run they tend to regret the
things they didn't do more than the things they did.[^S11]

**Say this to yourself:**
**"I can decide well without deciding perfectly."**

**If it keeps going:** Notice whether you're looking for a fact or a feeling. If you're
hoping the next search will make you feel sure, it probably won't. Big decisions rarely come
with certainty. They come with a choice and a plan.

**One small step:** Write your deadline on paper and tell one person, so it's real.

## Moment 21: Second-guessing a decision you already made

**The spiral:** Replay, wearing a Can't-Decide costume.

**What's going on:** You chose, and now your mind keeps checking: *Was it the right one?
What if I'd picked the other?* The decision is made, but the loop acts as if it's still
open. And while it's open, you can't settle into the choice you made.

There's an interesting finding here. In one study, photography students chose one of two
prints to keep. Students who could swap their choice later ended up liking their print
less than students whose choice was final.[^S12] Keeping the door open felt like a good
idea. It made people less happy with what they had.

**Try this:** The Good-Enough Rule, looking back.

1. Name it: **"Can't-Decide, after the fact."**
2. Remind yourself why you chose: read your must-haves. Did the choice meet them? Then it
   was a good-enough choice.
3. If you can still reverse it and there's a real, new reason to (a fact you didn't have
   before), decide once, by a deadline. If there's no new fact, stop checking.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"I made a good choice with what I knew. I'm done choosing."**

**If it keeps going:** Stop comparing. Unfollow the other apartment listings, close the
other job ads, shut the comparison tabs. Each look at the road not taken reopens the door.

**One small step:** Do one thing that commits you to the choice: empty one moving box, set up the
new account, tell someone the news.

## Moment 22: Asking everyone and still not knowing

**The spiral:** Can't-Decide.

**What's going on:** Asking for advice can help. But past a certain point, you're not asking
for information anymore. You're asking for someone to make the choice feel safe. Each
person gives a slightly different answer, which adds more to weigh, not less.

**Try this:** Limit the asking, then decide.

1. **Pick one or two people** whose judgment you trust on this kind of thing. Ask them a
   specific question, not "What would you do?" Try: **"Is there anything I'm missing?"**
2. **Then stop asking.** If you notice yourself drafting the question to a fourth or fifth
   person, that's the loop.
3. **Use the Good-Enough Rule** with what you have.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"More opinions won't make it feel safe. A deadline will make it done."**

**If it keeps going:** Try the coin trick. Assign each option a side and flip a coin. Before
you look, notice which side you're hoping for. That's often your answer. (You don't have to
do what the coin says.)

**One small step:** Send your one specific question to one trusted person, or, if you've
already asked enough, write your decision down.

---

## One Small Step

**Make one decision you've been putting off, today.** Pick something small or medium. Set
your must-haves, give yourself a deadline of tonight, and choose the first option that fits.
Then notice how it feels to have it done.

---

**POCKET CARD: THE DECISION RULE**

1. Three must-haves, at most.
2. A real deadline, with a time.
3. The first option that fits all three wins.
4. Then stop looking.
"Good enough, chosen, beats perfect, still searching."
