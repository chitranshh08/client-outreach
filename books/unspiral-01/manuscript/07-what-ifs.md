# Chapter 6: The What-Ifs

Dan is 38. On Monday, his company announced "a review of team structures." No details. No
dates. Just that sentence, in an email sent at 4:55 p.m.

By Monday night, Dan had worked out what he'd do if he lost his job, then what he'd do if
he couldn't find another one, then what would happen to the mortgage, then how he'd tell
his kids. By Tuesday morning, he'd done the whole chain three more times, each time a
little faster and a little darker. He hadn't learned anything new. He'd just practiced
being scared.

That's the What-If. It feels like getting ready, and some of it is. But after the first
pass, the What-If stops preparing you and starts rehearsing disasters that haven't
happened, most of which never will.

Remember what the What-If wants: **a plan for the likely outcome, and a place to park the
rest.** You can prepare for what's likely. You can't prepare for everything that's
possible. The cards in this chapter help you tell which is which.

---

## Moment 12: Before a big event or presentation

**The spiral:** What-If.

**What's going on:** Your mind is running every way it could go wrong: you'll forget your
words, the slides won't load, someone will ask a question you can't answer. Some of this is
useful. Most of it is the same three fears on repeat.

**Try this:** Best–Worst–Likely, then turn each worry into a task.

1. Write the worst, best and most likely outcomes.
2. Look at your list of fears. For each one, ask: *Is there one thing I can do about this
   in the next 24 hours?*
   - "I'll forget my words" → practice the opening twice, out loud.
   - "The slides won't work" → email them to yourself and bring a copy.
   - "Someone will ask something I don't know" → prepare the line **"Good question. Let me
     check and get back to you."**
3. Anything with no task attached gets parked.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"I've prepared for likely. That's enough."**

**If it keeps going:** On the day itself, change the channel. Don't spend the last hour
rereading your notes for the tenth time. Walk, eat something, talk to someone about
something else.

**One small step:** Pick one task from your list and do it now. Practicing the opening out
loud is a good first choice.

## Moment 13: "What if I lose my job?"

**The spiral:** What-If.

**What's going on:** Work worries can be real (layoffs, a bad review, a shaky company) or
vague (a cold email from your manager). Either way, your mind jumps to the end of the
story. It skips the steps in between, which is where all the things you could actually do
live.

**Try this:** Best–Worst–Likely, then a one-page "if it happens" plan.

- **Most likely:** What does the evidence actually say? A vague email, or a real
  announcement?
- **Worst case:** write it down. Then write the first three steps you'd take if it
  happened. (Update your résumé. Message two people in your field. Check what savings or
  support you'd have.)

Having the first steps written down makes the worst case feel less like a cliff and more
like a road you could walk.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"If it happens, I know my first step. Until then, it hasn't happened."**

**If it keeps going:** Park the rest in the Worry Window, and in the window, check for new
facts only. If nothing has changed since yesterday, you don't need to run the chain again.

**One small step:** Do the smallest step on your plan today. Opening your résumé and fixing one
line counts.

## Moment 14: Worrying about someone you love

*A partner driving late, a teenager out, a parent who lives alone*

**The spiral:** What-If.

**What's going on:** Love and worry travel together. When someone you care about is out of
reach, your mind fills the gap with every bad scene it can imagine. The worry feels like a
way of caring. It isn't a way of keeping them safe.

**Try this:** Solve or Park, with one agreed check-in.

1. Ask: is there one reasonable thing to do? Often there is. **"Text me when you get
   there."** Or a regular call with a parent. Agree on it ahead of time.
2. Then let the plan do the work. Between check-ins, the What-If gets parked: **"We have a
   plan. I'll know at 10."**
3. If the check-in is missed, take one calm step (a call, a text) before imagining the
   worst. Most missed check-ins are a dead phone.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"Worrying isn't protecting them. The plan is."**

**If it keeps going:** Change the channel with something that uses your hands and your
attention: cooking, a puzzle, folding laundry while listening to something. And remember
that a call that goes unanswered once isn't an emergency. Try again in ten minutes.

**One small step:** Set up the check-in now, for the next time this comes up.

## Moment 15: Money what-ifs

*Bills, a big expense, "what if we can't manage?"*

**The spiral:** What-If.

**What's going on:** Money worries are often worse in the dark. Not knowing the actual
numbers lets your mind fill in the scariest ones. This card won't give money advice. It's
a way to handle the worry, so you can think clearly about the money.

**Try this:** Solve or Park, starting with the real number.

1. **Look at the real number.** Open your bank app, or the bill, and write down the one
   figure you've been avoiding. Most money spirals run on guesses. A real number, even a
   bad one, gives you something to work with.
2. **Find one action for the next 24 hours.** Make a list of this month's fixed bills.
   Call a provider to ask about a payment plan. Book a time to sit down with your partner.
   If you'd like help, look for a free, reputable money advice service where you live.
3. **Park the big-picture worry** ("Will we ever be okay?") in the Worry Window.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"Numbers I can see are easier than numbers I imagine."**

**If it keeps going:** Watch for late-night money math. Doing sums in your head at 1 a.m.
almost never ends in a plan. Write "money: look at it at 10 a.m." on a note by the bed, and
use the Brain Dump from Chapter 3.

**One small step:** Write down the one number you've been avoiding. Just that.

## Moment 16: Health worries and the search spiral

**The spiral:** What-If.

**What's going on:** You notice a symptom, you search it, and the results include
something frightening. Now you search to make the frightening thing go away, and find
three more. Search results can't examine you. They show every possibility at once, the
rare ones next to the common ones, and a worried mind lands on the rare ones.

This card isn't medical advice. It's about the spiral. The symptom itself is something to
check with a professional.

**Try this:** Solve or Park, with a "one call, no searching" rule.

1. Ask the solve-or-park question. If you're worried about a symptom, the action is clear:
   **call your doctor or a nurse advice line and describe it.** Let someone who can examine
   you decide what it means.
2. Once that's booked, stop searching. Each search adds fuel, not answers.
3. Park the rest until the appointment: **"I've done the right thing. Now I wait for
   someone who knows."**

**If something feels urgent** (chest pain, trouble breathing, signs of a stroke, a sudden
severe symptom), don't wait for a window. **Call emergency services.**

**Say this to yourself:**
**"A search can't examine me. A doctor can."**

**If it keeps going:** If you notice that you check your body, search symptoms or ask for
reassurance about your health most days, even after a doctor has said you're fine, that's
worth mentioning to your doctor too. Chapter 10 has more on when to get help.

**One small step:** Make the call, or book the appointment. Then close every health tab
in your browser.

## Moment 17: Plans that might go wrong

*A trip, a party you're hosting, a big day*

**The spiral:** What-If.

**What's going on:** Anything with a lot of moving parts gives your mind a lot to worry
about: weather, traffic, guests, delays. Some planning is helpful. After that, the
What-If keeps running the list even when there's nothing left to plan.

**Try this:** Best–Worst–Likely, then a short backup list.

1. Write the three likeliest things to go wrong. Not all possible things. The likeliest.
2. Give each one a one-line backup. "If it rains, we eat inside." "If the train is late,
   I'll text the host." "If I forget something, I'll buy it there."
3. Anything that isn't on the list gets the line below.

**Say this to yourself:**
**"I have a backup for likely. I'll handle the rest if it happens."**

**If it keeps going:** Check the weather or the travel app once a day, not once an hour.
The forecast doesn't change because you look at it.

**One small step:** Write the three backups on a note, and put it with your tickets, your
packing list or your party plan.

---

## One Small Step

**Run Best–Worst–Likely on your biggest What-If this week, on paper.** Write all three
lines. Then make one plan for the most likely case, and one first step for the worst case.
Look at the worst case once more. It's often smaller in writing than it was in your head.
