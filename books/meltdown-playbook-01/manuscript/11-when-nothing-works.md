# Chapter 10: When Nothing Works: What to Do When the Script Falls Flat

Priya is four. For a whole week, her mom did everything in this book. The 60-Second Reset.
The 3-Line Script. Warnings before leaving the park. And on Friday, Priya still melted down
over her socks, loudly, for twenty minutes.

Her mom sat on the stairs afterward and thought, *It's not working. Maybe it works for other
kids, but not mine.*

If you've had a moment like that, this chapter is for you. Scripts are tools, not magic
spells. Some days they seem to bounce right off. That doesn't mean you're doing it wrong,
and it doesn't mean your child is broken. It usually means one of four things: the storm
needs time, the words need to get smaller, something underneath needs attention, or it's
time to ask for more help.

Let's look at each one.

## A script can "fail" and still be working

Here's something that's easy to miss: **the goal isn't zero meltdowns.** The goal is shorter
storms, quicker recovery, and a child who slowly learns to handle big feelings.

So when you judge whether things are working, don't ask "Did the meltdown stop?" Ask:

- Was it a little **shorter** than it used to be?
- Did my child **calm down faster** once it passed?
- Did it happen **less often** this week than last week?
- Did **I** stay calmer than I used to?

Any "yes" is progress. Change in young children is slow and uneven. There will be good weeks
and bad weeks, and a bad day doesn't erase the good ones.

This is where the meltdown tracker from Chapter 2 helps. Look back over a few weeks, not a
few days. Many parents find that things are improving even when it doesn't feel like it.

## Say less

When a script doesn't land, the most common reason is simple: **too many words.** A child in
a storm can't take in a paragraph, however kind it is.

If your script isn't working, cut it down. Then cut it down again.

- **Full script:** "You really wanted to keep playing. It's so hard to stop. It's time to
  go now. Do you want to walk or ride on my back?"
- **Shorter:** "You wanted to stay. Time to go. Walk or ride?"
- **Shortest:** "So hard. I'm here."

For children aged 2 to 3, the shortest version is often the best one. Pair it with calm
action: pick them up, hold them, move somewhere quieter. Your body does most of the talking.

The same goes for older children in a very big storm. When they're at the top of the
wave, say almost nothing. Stay close, stay quiet, and save the words for later.

## Check what's underneath

Sometimes meltdowns increase because something else in your child's life is hard. Before
you decide the scripts aren't working, look underneath.

**Go back to HALT (Chapter 2).** Is your child hungry, angry about something else, lonely,
or tired more often than usual? A change in sleep or meals can change everything.

**Look for big changes.** A move, a new baby, a new school or daycare, a parent traveling, an
illness, a family argument. Even happy changes can make children feel unsettled. It's
normal for meltdowns to rise during a big change and settle again afterward.

**Look at the pattern.** Do meltdowns happen at the same time, in the same place, or with the
same trigger? If so, you may be able to change the situation instead of handling the storm
every time.

## When it's too much: sensory overload

Some children feel noise, light, crowds, textures or busy places more strongly than others.
When there's too much coming in, their system gets overloaded, and a meltdown can follow,
often with no clear "reason."

Signs you might notice:

- Covering their ears or eyes
- Running away, hiding, or trying to get out of a busy space
- Melting down in loud or crowded places more than at home
- Being very bothered by clothing, tags, or certain food textures

What often helps:

- **Less input.** Move somewhere quieter and dimmer. Turn off music or screens.
- **Fewer words.** Say less, as above.
- **Space or pressure, whichever they like.** Some children want to be left alone; others calm
  down with a firm, snug hug or a heavy blanket. Follow your child's lead.
- **Planning ahead.** Shorter trips to busy places, a quiet break during parties, headphones
  for loud events if your child likes them.

Being sensitive to noise or textures isn't a diagnosis, and it isn't a problem to fix. It's
useful information about what your child needs. If sensory struggles are getting in the way
of daily life, like eating, dressing, sleeping or going out, mention it to your pediatrician.

## Adjust for age

The scripts in this book work across ages 2 to 7, but how you use them changes as your child
grows.

- **Ages 2–3:** Fewer words, more action. Short phrases, lots of comfort, and simple choices
  between two things you can hold up.
- **Ages 4–5:** More choices, more practice when calm. Role-play hard moments with toys, and
  let them help make the plan.
- **Ages 6–7:** More problem-solving together. Ask "What could we do next time?" and use
  their ideas, even if they're not perfect.

If a script feels too babyish or too complicated for your child, adjust it. You know your
child better than any book.

## When it's you who's struggling

Sometimes the script isn't working because *you* are running on empty. That's not a failure.
It's a sign you need support too.

- **Look after the basics:** sleep, food, a few minutes to yourself.
- **Share the load:** if you have a partner or family member nearby, take turns with the
  hardest moments.
- **Lower the bar on hard days:** some days, getting everyone fed and to bed safely is enough.

And if you often feel overwhelmed, hopeless, or unable to cope, please talk to your own doctor.
Parents' well-being matters too, and asking for help is a strong thing to do.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Most meltdowns in young children are a normal part of growing up. But some signs mean it's
worth getting a professional opinion. The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests talking to
your pediatrician if your child:[^S7]

- **Hurts themselves or others** during tantrums
- **Holds their breath and faints** during tantrums
- Has tantrums that **get worse after age 4**
- Seems to have **trouble expressing themselves with words** compared with other children
  their age

The CDC also advises talking with your child's doctor if your child **loses skills they once
had**, isn't meeting developmental milestones, or if you have any other concerns.[^S8]

Throughout this book, we've also mentioned a few other situations worth bringing up with
your pediatrician:

- Meltdowns that are **very frequent or very long** for your child's age, or that are **getting
  worse over several weeks** instead of better
- Meltdowns that seem to come with **no trigger at all**
- **Biting** that keeps happening at ages 6 to 7 (Chapter 5)
- **Fears or night fears** that get in the way of daily life or sleep for weeks (Chapters 7
  and 9)
- **Sensory struggles** that make everyday things like eating, dressing or going out very
  hard (this chapter)

And one more: **your own gut.** If something feels wrong to you, that's reason enough to ask.
You don't need to be sure. Pediatricians would much rather hear a question early than late.

### Making the most of the appointment

Bring your notes. The meltdown tracker from Chapter 2 is exactly the kind of information
that helps a doctor understand what's happening: how often, how long, what sets it off, and
what helps. Write down your biggest questions before you go, so you don't forget them in
the moment.

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Notice which change makes the biggest difference.**

For the next week, try one of the changes from this chapter:

1. **Say less:** cut your usual script down to its shortest version.
2. **Check underneath:** fix one HALT trigger, like an earlier bedtime or a snack before the
   hard moment.
3. **Less input:** move to a quieter spot at the first sign of a storm.

At the end of the week, write down which one helped most. That's your child's key, and it's
worth more than any script in this book.
