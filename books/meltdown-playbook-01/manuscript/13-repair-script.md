# Chapter 12: I Yelled. Now What? The Repair Script

It had been a long day. Finn's mom had worked late, dinner was burned, and Finn, who is five,
had spilled his milk for the second time. She heard herself shout, "WHAT is WRONG with you?
Why can't you be CAREFUL for once?"

Finn's face crumpled. He went quiet and slid off his chair.

That night, after he was asleep, she sat on the edge of his bed and felt sick. *I know better.
I've read the books. Why did I do that?*

If you've had a night like that, you're in very good company. Every parent loses it
sometimes. You're tired, you're human, and you're doing one of the hardest jobs there is.
This chapter isn't about never yelling again. It's about what you do *after*.

## Why repair matters

A moment of yelling doesn't undo everything else you've given your child. But what happens
next matters a lot.

When you come back to your child and make things right, you teach them several things at
once:

- **Relationships can survive hard moments.** People who love each other can get upset and
  still be okay.
- **Everyone makes mistakes, including grown-ups.** Your child learns that mistakes can be
  fixed, which makes their own mistakes less scary.
- **How to apologize.** You can't teach a child to say sorry well by telling them to. You teach
  it by doing it.
- **Their feelings matter.** When you notice the hurt you caused and name it, you show your
  child that their feelings are important to you.

Some parents worry that apologizing makes them look weak. It's the opposite. A calm,
honest apology shows your child what strength looks like: taking responsibility without
making excuses.

## The Repair Script

A good repair has three parts. You can say them in less than a minute.

**1. Name what you did.**
**"I yelled at you at dinner."**

Be specific and honest. No need for a long speech.

**2. Say it wasn't okay, without excuses.**
**"That wasn't okay. It's not your fault that I yelled. I was tired and frustrated, and I
didn't handle it well."**

Notice the difference between explaining and excusing. "I was tired" explains. "I yelled
because you spilled your milk" puts the blame on your child. Keep the responsibility with
you.

**3. Say what you'll do next time.**
**"Next time I feel that frustrated, I'm going to take a big breath before I talk."**

This part matters most. It shows your child that you're learning too, and it gives you a
plan for yourself.

Here's the full script together:

> **"I yelled at you at dinner. That wasn't okay, and it's not your fault. I was tired and
> I didn't handle it well. Next time I feel that frustrated, I'm going to take a big breath
> first. I love you."**

Then give them space to respond. Some children want a hug. Some want to tell you how it
felt. Some just say "okay" and go back to playing. All of those are fine.

## What to avoid in a repair

**"I'm sorry, but you…"** The word "but" cancels the apology. Keep the repair about what *you* did.
If there's something to talk about with your child's behavior, do it separately, later.

**Making it about your guilt.** "I'm such a terrible mom, I'm so sorry, I always ruin
everything." Your child may end up comforting *you*, which puts a heavy job on small
shoulders. Keep it short and steady.

**Asking them to say it's okay.** "Do you forgive me?" puts pressure on your child. Let them
respond however they want.

**Waiting forever.** You don't need a perfect moment. Repair works best within the same day,
once everyone is calm, but a repair the next morning is still worth doing.

## Repair by age

- **Ages 2–3:** Keep it very short and physical. Get down to their level: "I yelled. I'm sorry.
  I love you." Then a hug or a snuggle.
- **Ages 4–5:** Use the full three-part script. Ask one simple question: "How did it feel
  when I yelled?" Listen without defending yourself.
- **Ages 6–7:** You can talk a little more. Share what you'll try next time and ask for their
  idea: "What could help us both when we're frustrated?" Some children love having a
  family signal, like a hand on the heart, that means "let's both take a breath."

## Repair when your child is the one who lost it

The same script works in reverse. After your child hits, yells or says something hurtful,
you can help them repair too, once they're calm.

Don't force a quick "say sorry." Instead, guide them through the same three parts:

- **What happened?** "You threw the block, and it hit your sister."
- **How can we help?** "She's hurt. What could make her feel better?"
- **Next time?** "Next time you're that mad, what could you do?" (The safe mad list from
  Chapter 5 is perfect here.)

When children see you repair your own mistakes, they learn to repair theirs much more
naturally.

## Letting go of the guilt

After a hard moment, many parents replay it for hours. Guilt can be useful for a little
while, because it tells you something mattered to you. But carrying it for days doesn't
help you or your child.

Here's a simple way to put it down:

1. **Repair** with your child, using the script above.
2. **Learn one thing.** What set you off? Were you hungry, tired, rushed? What's one thing you
   could change? (HALT from Chapter 2 works for parents too.)
3. **Let it go.** Say to yourself what you'd say to a friend: *"That was a hard moment. I
   repaired it. I'm still a good parent."*

You're not aiming to be a parent who never yells. You're aiming to be a parent who comes
back and makes it right. That's something you can do every single time.

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Use the repair script once this week.**

The next time you lose your temper, even a little, come back once everyone is calm and use
the three parts:

1. Name what you did.
2. Say it wasn't okay, with no excuses.
3. Say what you'll do next time.

Afterward, notice how your child responds. You may be surprised how quickly your child
moves on, and how close you feel afterward.
