# Chapter 8: "That's Mine!": Siblings, Sharing and Keeping the Peace

Rosie is six and Ben is three. Rosie built a castle out of blocks. Ben knocked it down. Rosie
shoved Ben. Ben screamed. Both of them ran to their dad at the same time, both shouting
"It's not FAIR!"

Their dad had about two seconds to decide who was right. Ben started it, but Rosie pushed.
Rosie is older and "should know better," but Ben knocked down her castle on purpose. Whatever
he decided, one child would feel it was unfair.

Here's the relief: **you don't have to be the judge.** Most of the time, deciding who started
it makes things worse, because someone always loses. This chapter gives you a different
job. Instead of judge, you become the **sportscaster**.

## Be a sportscaster, not a judge

A sports commentator describes what's happening without taking sides. You can do the same
with sibling fights. Describe what you see, calmly and without blame, then hand the problem
back to the children to solve, with your help.

- **Judge:** "Ben, you knocked it down on purpose, so you're in trouble."
- **Sportscaster:** "The castle got knocked down. Rosie, you're really upset. Ben, you're crying
  because Rosie pushed you. That's two upset kids. Let's figure this out."

When you describe instead of judging, both children feel seen, nobody feels blamed, and you
avoid getting stuck in the "who started it" trap. Safety still comes first: if anyone is
being hurt, block and separate before you say anything (Chapter 5).

## Sharing isn't automatic

Adults often expect young children to share instantly. But sharing means giving up
something you want *right now*, which takes exactly the kind of self-control that's still
being built (Chapter 2).

That's why this book uses **turn-taking** instead of forced sharing. With turn-taking, each
child gets to finish their turn, and the next child knows their turn is coming. It's easier
to wait when you know the wait will end.

## Moment 21: The toy grab

**What's going on:** One child has something the other wants, so they take it. Young
children act on wanting before they think about the other person's feelings.

**Say this:**
**"You really want the truck. Ben is using it now. I won't let you grab it. You can ask,
'Can I have a turn when you're done?'"**

**If it gets bigger:** Hold the toy yourself for a moment if you need to. **"I'm going to hold
the truck until everyone is calm."** Then help both children: **"Ben, you can finish your turn.
Then it's Rosie's turn. I'll help you remember."**

**Instead of:** "Share with your brother right now!"
**Try:** "When you're done with your turn, it's his turn."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Offer a swap: "Here's another truck you can use while you wait." Toddlers accept a
  replacement more easily than a wait.
- **4–5:** Use a timer for turns: "When the timer beeps, it's Rosie's turn."
- **6–7:** Let them negotiate: "You both want it. What's your plan?" Step in only if the plan
  isn't fair to both.

**Next time:** Before a playdate, put away a few very special toys that your child doesn't
have to share. It prevents fights over the things they care most about.

## Moment 22: "It's not fair!"

**What's going on:** Children watch closely for fairness, especially between siblings. Often
"it's not fair" really means "I'm worried I matter less."

**Say this:**
**"It feels unfair to you. You wish you had one too. In our family, everyone gets what they
need, even if it's not always the same thing. Do you want to tell me more about what feels
unfair?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Don't argue about whether it's fair. Listen. **"You feel like your sister
got more. That's a hard feeling."** Often a child who feels heard lets go of the complaint.

**Instead of:** "Life isn't fair, get used to it."
**Try:** "It feels unfair. Tell me about it."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Keep it simple: "You want a cookie too. Here's your cookie."
- **4–5:** Use the language of turns: "Today was her turn to pick the park. Tomorrow is your turn."
- **6–7:** Talk about the difference between equal and fair: "Your brother gets help with his
  shoes because he's three. You get to stay up later because you're six. That's fair, even
  though it's not the same."

**Next time:** Spend a little one-on-one time with each child every day (see this chapter's
action step). Many "not fair" complaints fade when each child feels they get their own
share of you.

## Moment 23: When siblings hit each other

**What's going on:** Brothers and sisters live close together, share space and toys, and
fight over both. Hitting between siblings is common, but it still needs a firm limit every
time.

**Say this:**
*(Separate them first. Check on the hurt child.)*
**"I won't let you hit each other. You're both really mad. Let's take a break: you sit
here, you sit there, until your bodies are calm. Then we'll talk."**

**If it gets bigger:** Keep them apart and stay calm. Use the sportscaster approach to describe
what happened without blame. **"It looks like you both wanted the same seat, and then
there was hitting."** Help each child calm down, and use the safe mad list (Chapter 5).

**Instead of:** "Who started it?"
**Try:** "What happened? Let's hear from each of you."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Stay very close when a toddler plays with an older sibling. Block early, and
  teach the older child to call you instead of hitting back.
- **4–5:** Practice words for sibling problems when everyone is calm: "Stop, I don't like
  that!" "I'm using this."
- **6–7:** Have them help make the plan: "What can we do so this doesn't happen again?"
  Write down their ideas.

**Next time:** Notice when sibling fights happen. Many happen when children are hungry,
tired, or bored. A snack, a change of activity, or some time apart often prevents the next
one.

## Moment 24: Jealous of the new baby

**What's going on:** A new baby changes everything for an older child. They may feel pushed
aside, worried about losing your love, or just confused. Jealousy can show up as anger,
clinginess, or acting "like a baby" again.

**Say this:**
**"It's hard having a new baby in the house. Sometimes you wish it was just you and me.
I love you so much, and that will never change. Do you want to read a book together, just
us two?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Take their feelings seriously, even when they say "I don't like the
baby." **"You don't like the baby right now. That's a real feeling. I won't let you hurt the
baby. You can tell me when you feel mad."** Then give them some of your attention, even a
few minutes.

**Instead of:** "You should love your baby brother!"
**Try:** "It's okay to have mixed feelings about the baby."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Expect some going backward, like wanting a bottle or a pacifier again. Stay
  patient; it usually passes.
- **4–5:** Give them a "big kid" job with the baby, like choosing the baby's socks or singing to
  the baby, if they want to.
- **6–7:** Tell them what makes them special: "You can do so many things the baby can't.
  Want to stay up ten minutes later tonight and read with me?"

**Next time:** Schedule time alone with your older child, even ten minutes a day. Name it
something special, and protect it. It reassures them more than any words can.

---

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Give each child five minutes of one-on-one "special time" today.**

1. Pick a time when you can give one child your full attention. Put your phone in another
   room, and save the chores and the other children for afterward.
2. Let your child choose the activity. Play what they want, the way they want.
3. Give it a name, like "special time" or "just us time," so they know it's theirs.
4. Try to do it every day, even if it's only five minutes.

Children who feel they get enough of you have less reason to fight their siblings for it.
