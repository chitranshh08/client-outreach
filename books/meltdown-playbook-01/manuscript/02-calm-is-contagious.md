# Chapter 1: Calm Is Contagious, So Catch It First

Maya is three. Her toast was cut into rectangles, and she wanted triangles. Now the toast
is on the floor and Maya is screaming.

Her dad feels the heat rise in his chest. "Maya. MAYA. Stop it. It's just toast!" His
voice gets louder. Maya's gets louder too. Within a minute they are both yelling, and
nobody has eaten breakfast.

Rewind the scene. This time, before he says a word, Maya's dad takes one slow breath out.
He crouches down. He lowers his voice. "You wanted triangles. I'm right here." Maya still
cries, but the crying starts to slow down instead of speeding up.

Same toast. Same child. Same dad. The difference was what happened in his body in the
first few seconds.

## Your child borrows your calm

Young children can't calm themselves down yet. The brain skills they need for that are
still being built, and they will keep developing for years (Chapter 2 explains this).

Until those skills are ready, children rely on the adults around them. Researchers who
study self-regulation call this **co-regulation**: a caring adult helps a child calm down,
again and again, and over time the child learns to do it on their own.[^S3]

In plain words, **your child borrows your nervous system.** When you get louder, faster
and tenser, your child's storm tends to grow. When you get slower, lower and softer, you
give them something steady to hold on to.

This is good news. It means the most powerful tool you have in a meltdown isn't a clever
sentence. It's your own calm. And calm is a skill you can practice.

## The 60-Second Reset

This is what you do in the first minute of a meltdown, *before* you try to fix anything.
It has four steps. You can do all four while your child is still screaming.

**1. Plant your feet.**
Stop moving. Feel both feet on the floor. If you can, crouch down so you're closer to
your child's height. This tells your own body: *I'm not running, and I'm not attacking.*

**2. Breathe out slowly.**
Take one breath in, then let it out slowly, longer than the breath in. Count to five as
you breathe out if that helps. Do it two or three times. Focus on the long breath out.

**3. Soften your face.**
Unclench your jaw. Relax your forehead. Let your shoulders drop. Your child reads your
face faster than your words, and a soft face says *you're safe with me.*

**4. Drop your voice.**
When you speak, speak lower and slower than you want to. If your child is loud, go quiet.
A quiet voice is hard to argue with.

That's the whole reset. It takes about a minute, and nobody else needs to notice you're
doing it.

### "But I don't have 60 seconds!"

Sometimes you don't. If your child is about to run into the street or hit the baby,
**safety comes first.** Move, block, pick them up. Then do the reset once everyone is safe.

In every other meltdown, you almost always have one minute. The toast will wait. The
screen can stay on for sixty more seconds. The storm is not an emergency, even when it
sounds like one.

## The 3-Line Meltdown Script

Once you've done the reset, you need something to say. Most parents grab whatever comes
out first: "Stop crying." "Calm down." "You're being ridiculous." Those words are
understandable, but they tend to add fuel.

Here is what to say instead. Learn these three lines and you'll have something useful to
say in almost any meltdown:

**"I see you're ___."**
**"I'm right here."**
**"I won't let you ___."**

Let's look at each line.

**Line 1: "I see you're ___."** Name what you see. *"I see you're really mad." "I see
you're so disappointed." "I see you wanted triangles."* You aren't agreeing that the
toast is a tragedy. You're showing your child that you noticed what's happening inside
them.

**Line 2: "I'm right here."** Tell them they're not alone. A child in a meltdown feels out
of control, and that is scary. This line says *the adult is still in charge, and the
adult is staying.*

**Line 3: "I won't let you ___."** Hold the limit, calmly. *"I won't let you throw food."
"I won't let you hit." "I won't let you run into the parking lot."* Notice that it's
about what **you** will do, not an order your child has to follow. That's why it works
even when they're too upset to listen.

You don't always need line 3. If your child is crying but not doing anything unsafe or
off-limits, lines 1 and 2 are often enough.

### The script in action

Here's how it sounds in a few common moments.

*Leaving the playground:*
**"I see you're sad we have to go. I'm right here. I won't let you run away from me in the
parking lot."**

*Screens off:*
**"I see you're really mad the show ended. I'm right here. I won't let you throw the
remote."**

*A sibling took a toy:*
**"I see you're so frustrated. I'm right here. I won't let you hit your brother."**

Then stop talking. This is the hardest part. After the three lines, **stay close and stay
quiet.** A flood of extra words is more for the adult than the child. Let your calm do the
work.

### What happens next

The storm usually doesn't stop the moment you say the script. That's okay. You aren't
trying to switch the feelings off. You're trying to help your child move through them
faster, with you beside them.

Sometimes it looks like this:

- The screaming continues, but it slowly gets quieter.
- Your child crawls into your lap, or lets you rub their back.
- They look at you and start talking, even if it's to complain.

Any of those means the script is working. When your child is calm enough to talk, *then*
you can problem-solve, talk about what happened, or move on with your day. Chapter 3
shows you exactly how.

## Why your child might push back

The first few times you use the reset and the script, your child may seem to melt down
*harder*. Some children test a new response to see if it's real. Others are surprised
that the grown-up didn't yell, and they aren't sure what to do with that.

Keep going. Use the same words for at least a week. Most children settle into a new
pattern once they see that it doesn't change.

## When you're the one about to lose it

Some days, you'll feel your own storm rising and the reset won't feel like enough. That's
human. Here are two extra moves for those days:

**Name it out loud.** *"I'm feeling really frustrated right now. I'm going to take a big
breath."* Then do it. You're calming yourself and showing your child how it's done.

**Take a safe pause.** If your child is safe, it's okay to step a few feet away. *"I need
a minute to calm my body. I'll be right here."* Stay where your child can see you. A short
pause is much better than an explosion.

If you do explode, don't panic. Chapter 12 shows you how to repair it, and repair is one
of the most useful things your child can learn from you.

## Your 5-Minute Action

1. **Practice the 60-Second Reset twice today, while things are calm.** Standing at the
   sink, waiting for the kettle, sitting in the car. Practicing when it's easy makes it
   automatic when it's hard.
2. **Write the 3-Line Meltdown Script somewhere you'll see it.** A sticky note on the
   fridge, the lock screen of your phone, or the tear-out card below.
3. **Use the script at the next meltdown.** Just once. Notice what happens, without
   judging yourself.

That's it. You now have a plan for the first minute of any meltdown.
