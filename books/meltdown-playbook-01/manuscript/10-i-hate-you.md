# Chapter 9: "I Hate You!": Big Disappointments, Big Fears, Big Words

Owen is five. He was losing a board game to his grandpa, and when the last card went the
wrong way, he swept the pieces onto the floor and shouted, "I hate this game! I hate YOU!"

His mom, watching from the kitchen, felt her stomach drop. *Did he really just say that?*

Words like "I hate you" hit hard. So do tears over a broken toy that seems tiny to us, or
panic about a dog on the sidewalk. These moments are different from the battles in earlier
chapters. There's often no rule being broken and nothing to hold firm on. There's just a
child with a feeling too big to carry.

This chapter is about those feelings: disappointment, fear, and the big, hurtful words that
come out when a child doesn't have better ones yet. Your main job here isn't to set a limit.
It's to **stay steady, name the feeling, and help your child carry it**, without rushing to
fix it or taking the words personally.

## Comfort before fixing

When a child is disappointed, most parents rush to make it better: glue the toy, promise a
new one, find the bright side. It comes from love. But fixing too fast can send a message
you don't mean: *this feeling is a problem, so let's get rid of it quickly.*

Try this order instead:

1. **Feel it with them.** "Oh no. Your tower fell."
2. **Let the feeling finish.** Stay close. Don't hurry it.
3. **Then fix, if it needs fixing.** "Do you want to build it again, or take a break?"

A child who learns that disappointment can be felt and survived is learning one of the most
useful skills there is.

## Moment 25: Losing a game

**What's going on:** Young children want to win, and they haven't learned yet that losing
is survivable. Losing feels like failing, and failing feels awful.

**Say this:**
**"You really wanted to win. Losing feels so bad. I won't let you throw the pieces. You can
stomp it out, and then we can play again or do something else."**

**If it gets bigger:** Pause the game. **"Let's take a break from the game until your body is
calm."** Stay close and use the 3-Line Script. Come back to the game only when they're ready.

**Instead of:** "It's just a game. Stop being a sore loser."
**Try:** "Losing is hard. Everyone feels that way sometimes."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Choose games where everyone plays together, like building or matching, rather than
  games with a winner.
- **4–5:** Practice losing on purpose yourself, out loud: "Oh, I lost! I feel disappointed.
  I'll take a breath. Good game!"
- **6–7:** Talk afterward about being a good sport: "What can you say when you lose?" Practice
  "Good game" together.

**Next time:** Play games with a lot of luck and a little skill, where anyone can win, and
narrate your own losses calmly. Children learn more from watching you lose well than from
being told how to lose.

## Moment 26: The broken toy (or broken tower)

**What's going on:** To your child, the toy wasn't just a toy. It was something they loved,
built, or planned with. When it breaks, they may grieve it as a real loss.

**Say this:**
**"Oh no. Your tower fell down. You worked so hard on it. That's really sad. I'm right
here."**

**If it gets bigger:** Let them cry. Resist the urge to fix or replace right away. **"It's okay
to be sad about it. Take all the time you need."** When they're calmer: **"Do you want to
build it again, or save that for tomorrow?"**

**Instead of:** "Don't cry, we'll get you a new one."
**Try:** "You really loved that. It's sad when things break."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Hold them and name it simply: "Broken. So sad." Then offer comfort.
- **4–5:** Help them decide what to do next: fix it, throw it away, or keep the pieces.
- **6–7:** Talk about how to protect special things next time: "Where could we keep your
  towers safe from little brother?"

**Next time:** Give your child a safe place for special creations, like a shelf the younger
siblings can't reach.

---

## Moment 27: When plans get cancelled

**What's going on:** Your child was looking forward to something: a playdate, the pool, a
visit to Grandma. Now it's not happening. Disappointment plus a change of plan is a hard
mix for young children.

**Say this:**
**"You were so excited about the pool. Now we can't go. That's really disappointing. Let's
put 'pool' on the calendar for another day. What should we do today instead?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Stay with the feeling before moving to the plan. **"You really wanted to go.
It's okay to be upset. I'm right here."** Only offer the alternative once they're a bit calmer.

**Instead of:** "Stop whining, it's not the end of the world."
**Try:** "You were really looking forward to it. That's hard."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Distract gently after the first wave of sadness: "Let's do water play in the bath
  instead!"
- **4–5:** Let them help choose a new plan from two options.
- **6–7:** Mark the new date on a calendar together, so the plan feels real.

**Next time:** When plans might change, say so ahead of time: "We're hoping to go to the pool,
but if it rains, we'll go another day." A warning makes disappointment easier to handle.

## Moment 28: Tears at daycare drop-off

**What's going on:** Saying goodbye to a parent is hard, especially for young children. Your
child may cry, cling, or beg you to stay. These tears are about missing you, not about
daycare being bad.

**Say this:**
**"You don't want me to go. You'll miss me. I always come back. I'll pick you up after
snack time. Give me a big hug, and then I'm going."**

**If it gets bigger:** Keep the goodbye short and warm. Hand your child to a caregiver you trust,
say goodbye clearly, and leave. **"I love you. I'll be back after snack time."** A long goodbye
usually makes leaving harder for both of you. It's fine to call the daycare later to check
how they settled.

**Instead of:** Sneaking out while they aren't looking.
**Try:** A clear, short goodbye, every time. Sneaking out can leave a child wondering when
you'll disappear next.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Use the same goodbye ritual every day: a hug, a kiss, a wave at the window.
- **4–5:** Give them something of yours to hold, like a photo or a scarf: "Keep this until I
  come back."
- **6–7:** Talk the night before about what they'll do at school or daycare tomorrow.

**Next time:** Practice short separations at home, and always come back when you said you would.
Every time you return as promised, your child learns to trust the goodbye a little more.

## Moment 29: Scared of the dark, dogs or the doctor

**What's going on:** Fears are a normal part of growing up. As a child's imagination grows,
so does their ability to picture scary things. The fear feels real to them, even when
there's nothing dangerous.

**Say this:**
**"You feel scared of the dog. That's okay. I'm right here, and I'll keep you safe. Do you
want to hold my hand, or stand behind me?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Don't push them toward the thing they fear. Move to a place where they
feel safe, and stay calm. **"We can stay over here. You're safe with me."** Once they're calm,
talk about it together.

**Instead of:** "Don't be a baby, it's just a dog."
**Try:** "You feel scared. That's okay. I'll help you."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Stay close and let them watch from a safe distance.
- **4–5:** Use picture books about the thing they fear. Many children feel braver after
  reading about it.
- **6–7:** Help them take small brave steps, like waving at a friendly dog from far away, then
  a little closer another day.

**Next time:** Talk about fears during calm times. Let your child ask questions and draw or
play out the scary thing. For the doctor, role-play a check-up with a toy doctor kit. If a
fear gets in the way of daily life for weeks, mention it to your pediatrician (Chapter 10).

## Moment 30: "I hate you!"

*And "You're the worst mom ever!"*

**What's going on:** "I hate you" is usually a child's way of saying "I'm so angry and I don't
have a better word for it." It's not a real statement about your relationship. It's a
feeling, spoken out loud, in the only words they could find.

**Say this:**
**"You're really, really mad at me. You wanted to keep playing and I said no. I love you
even when you're mad at me."**

**If it gets bigger:** Stay steady. Don't take the bait. **"I can hear how mad you are. I'm
not going anywhere."** Later, when they're calm, you can talk about better words: **"When
you're that mad, you can say 'I'm SO mad at you!' That's okay to say."**

**Instead of:** "That's a horrible thing to say. Go to your room!"
**Try:** "You're really mad at me. I still love you."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** At this age it may come out as "No! Go away!" instead. Same response: stay calm
  and name the feeling.
- **4–5:** Offer better words: "You can say 'I'm really mad at you.'" Practice it when calm.
- **6–7:** Talk afterward about how words can hurt: "When you said that, it hurt my feelings.
  What could you say next time?"

**Next time:** Model saying your own big feelings out loud in a safe way: "I'm really
frustrated right now. I'm going to take a breath." Children learn angry words from watching
how adults handle anger.

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Read one feelings picture book together this week.**

1. Choose a picture book about emotions. Your local library will have plenty; ask the
   librarian for books about feelings, anger, or being scared.
2. Read it at a calm time, not during a meltdown.
3. As you read, ask: "How do you think she feels?" "Have you ever felt like that?"
4. Refer back to it later: "Remember the bear who was so mad? What did he do?"

Stories give children a safe way to practice feelings before the next storm arrives.
