# Part 3: Fewer Storms Ahead

*Parts 1 and 2 helped you get through meltdowns. Part 3 helps you have fewer of them.*

# Chapter 11: Feelings School: Teaching Calm When Everyone Is Calm

It's Sunday morning. Pancakes are on the table, nobody is tired, and nobody is crying. Nora,
who is four, is happily drowning her pancake in syrup.

Her dad picks up a pancake and makes a sad face. "Uh-oh. This pancake is feeling… hmm. What
do you think he's feeling?"

Nora giggles. "Grumpy! He's GRUMPY because you're going to eat him!"

They spend the next two minutes giving pancakes feelings. Happy pancake. Scared pancake.
Frustrated pancake that can't find the syrup.

It looks like silliness. But Nora is doing something important. She's practicing feelings
words, noticing faces, and learning that feelings have names, all at a time when her
upstairs brain is fully switched on and ready to learn.

That's the big idea of this chapter: **the best time to teach calm is when everyone is
already calm.** During a meltdown, your child can't learn much (Chapter 2). But in the easy
moments, over breakfast, in the bath, on the drive to school, they can learn a lot. Think of
it as Feelings School, in five-minute lessons.

## Lesson 1: Words for feelings

A child who can say "I'm frustrated" has another choice besides throwing the puzzle. The
more feelings words your child knows, the more ways they have to let a feeling out.

Here are some words to build up over time. Don't try to teach them all at once. Pick one or
two a week and use them often.

**Ages 2–3: the basics**
Happy, sad, mad, scared, tired, hungry, silly.

**Ages 4–5: adding more**
Frustrated, disappointed, worried, excited, proud, lonely, jealous, calm, grumpy, shy.

**Ages 6–7: finer shades**
Nervous, embarrassed, annoyed, furious, hurt, confused, left out, overwhelmed, relieved,
grateful.

**How to teach them:**

- **Name your own feelings out loud.** "I'm feeling frustrated because the car won't start.
  I'm going to take a big breath." Your child learns the word and a way to handle it.
- **Name feelings in books and shows.** "Look at the bear's face. How do you think he feels?"
- **Name your child's feelings when they're mild.** Not just in big storms. "You look proud of
  that drawing!" "You seem a little nervous about the party."

## Lesson 2: Feelings games

Children learn best through play. These games take a few minutes and need nothing but you.

**Feelings charades.** Take turns acting out a feeling with your face and body, while the
other person guesses. Mad stomping, sad slumping, excited jumping. Children love it when
the grown-up does a very dramatic "grumpy."

**The inside weather report.** Ask your child: "What's the weather like inside you today?"
Sunny means happy, cloudy means a bit sad, stormy means mad, foggy means confused or tired.
Share your own weather too. It gives children an easy way to talk about feelings that are
hard to name.

**Feelings faces.** Draw simple faces together (happy, sad, mad, scared) and stick them on the
fridge. Your child can point to the one that matches how they feel, even when words are
hard to find.

**What would you do?** Tell a tiny story: "A little rabbit wanted the red crayon, but his
friend had it. How do you think the rabbit felt? What could he do?" Let your child come up
with ideas. It's practice for their own hard moments.

## Lesson 3: Kid-sized breathing

In Chapter 1, you learned to calm your own body with a slow breath out. Children can learn a
version of this too, but "take a deep breath" is too abstract for most young children. Make
it something they can see and do.

**Blow out the candle.** Hold up one finger like a birthday candle. Breathe in, then blow it out
slowly. Try "blowing out" five candles, one finger at a time.

**Smell the flower, blow out the candle.** Pretend to hold a flower in one hand and a candle in
the other. Breathe in slowly to smell the flower. Breathe out slowly to blow out the candle.

**Dragon breath.** Pretend to be a dragon. Breathe in slowly through the nose, then breathe out
a long, slow "fire breath" through the mouth. Mad children often love this one, because it
lets them be big and fierce while their breathing slows down. (This is the dragon breath
from the comfort choices in Chapter 3.)

**Belly buddy.** Your child lies down with a stuffed animal on their tummy and watches it rise
and fall as they breathe slowly. It's a lovely one for bedtime (Chapter 7).

**Practice when calm, use when upset.** Practice these for fun at calm times, a few times a
week. Then, when a storm starts, you can say: **"Let's do dragon breath together."** A skill
your child has practiced for fun is much easier to use when they're upset.

## Lesson 4: A calm-down corner

A calm-down corner is a cozy spot in your home where anyone can go when feelings get big.
It's not a punishment and not a time-out. It's a place to calm down, and it works best
when you go there *with* your child, especially at first.

You can set one up in about 20 minutes.

**Choose the spot.** A quiet corner of the living room, a nook in their bedroom, a tent, or
even a big cardboard box with a blanket inside. Somewhere a little away from the action.

**Make it cozy.** A soft cushion or beanbag, a blanket, a favorite stuffed animal.

**Add calming things.** Pick a few from this list:

- Something to squeeze: a stress ball or a soft toy
- Something to look at: a glitter jar, picture books about feelings
- Something for their hands: play dough, a fidget toy, paper and crayons
- The feelings faces from Lesson 2
- Your family's safe mad list (Chapter 5)

**Introduce it at a calm time.** "This is our calm-down corner. When you feel mad or sad or
too-much, you can come here. I'll come with you if you want." Let them help set it up. A
corner your child built is one they'll actually want to use.

**Use it yourself.** When you're stressed, say: "I'm feeling frustrated. I'm going to sit in the
calm-down corner for a minute." Children copy what they see.

**Keep it positive.** Never send a child to the calm-down corner as a punishment. If it
becomes a place of shame, it stops working as a place of calm.

## Lesson 5: Talk about the storm afterward

You already know the after-the-storm talk from Chapter 3: What happened? How did you feel?
What could we do next time? This is Feelings School too. Every time you have this short,
warm conversation after a meltdown, your child learns to understand their own storms a
little better.

Over time, you may notice your child starting to do it on their own: "I was so mad because
he took my car. Next time I'll say stop." That's the goal of this whole book, arriving in
small steps.
