# Chapter 7: Out the Door and Into Bed: Mornings and Bedtimes Without the War

Jack is six. It's 7:50 am, school starts at 8:15, and he's lying on the hallway floor in one
sock, building a Lego spaceship. His mom has asked him to put his shoes on four times.
The fifth time, she yells. Jack cries. They both arrive late and grumpy.

Twelve hours later, it happens again in reverse. Now Jack won't stop. One more story, one
more drink of water, one more question about sharks. His mom, who has been "on" since
6:30 am, finally snaps.

Mornings and bedtimes are hard for the same reason: **they're full of transitions, and they
happen when everyone is short on time or energy.** In the morning, you're rushing. At night,
you're exhausted. Your child feels both.

The good news is that these moments happen at the same time, in the same way, every
day. That makes them the easiest ones to plan for. The secret weapon for both is a
**routine**: the same steps, in the same order, every day, so your child knows what comes
next without you having to push.

## Why routines calm things down

When your child knows exactly what comes next, there's less to argue about. The routine
becomes the boss, not you. "What's next on the chart?" is a question. "Put your shoes on!"
for the fifth time is a fight.

You'll make a picture routine chart with your child at the end of this chapter. For now,
here are the five moments where mornings and bedtimes most often fall apart.

---

## Moment 16: "I don't want to get up!"

**What's going on:** Your child is still tired, cozy, and not ready for the day to start.
Some children wake slowly and feel grumpy for the first few minutes, much like adults.

**Say this:**
**"You're so cozy. It's hard to get out of a warm bed. It's time to get up now. Do you want
to hop out like a frog or roll out like a log?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Keep it light and physical. Open the curtains, turn on a light, and give
them a few minutes. **"I'll be back in two minutes, and then it's time for feet on the
floor."** Come back when you said you would, and help them up calmly.

**Instead of:** "Get up NOW, we're going to be late!"
**Try:** "Good morning. Two more minutes of snuggles, then feet on the floor."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** A cuddle and a song usually works better than words. Carry them to breakfast if
  you need to.
- **4–5:** Make it a game: "Can you get to the kitchen before I finish counting to ten?"
- **6–7:** Try an alarm clock they can set themselves. Being woken by a clock often
  causes fewer fights than being woken by a parent.

**Next time:** Look at bedtime. A child who fights getting up is often a child who went to
sleep too late. Moving bedtime earlier by 15 minutes can change the whole morning.

---

## Moment 17: Shoes, coat, teeth: the morning rush

**What's going on:** You're watching the clock. Your child isn't. Young children live in the
moment, and "we're going to be late" doesn't mean much to them yet. Your stress rises, and
they feel it without understanding it.

**Say this:**
**"You're busy building your spaceship. It's hard to stop. It's time for shoes. Do you want
to put them on at the door or on the stairs?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Get close and help. Rushing children with words rarely works, but
helping with your hands does. **"I'll put one shoe on, and you do the other."** Stay calm even
if you're late. Being five minutes late calmly is better than being on time after a
screaming match.

**Instead of:** "Hurry up! Why are you always so slow?"
**Try:** "What's next on the chart?"

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Do most of it for them, with them helping one small part. "You push the arm through."
- **4–5:** Race a timer or a song: "Can you get your shoes on before the song ends?"
- **6–7:** Let them run their own routine from the chart, and only step in when they're stuck.

**Next time:** Do as much as you can the night before: clothes laid out, bags packed, shoes by the
door. And wake up 10 minutes earlier than you think you need. Most morning meltdowns
are really time pressure meltdowns.

---

## Moment 18: "Just one more story!"

*Bedtime stalling*

**What's going on:** Your child wants more time with you, more fun, or just to put off the
dark and quiet. Stalling is often about connection: bedtime is when they have your full
attention, and they don't want it to end.

**Say this:**
**"You love story time. You wish it could go on and on. That was the last story for
tonight. Do you want a big hug or a tiny squeeze before lights out?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Stay kind and boring. **"Story time is finished. I'll see you in the
morning."** If they call out, respond briefly and calmly each time, without starting a new
conversation. **"It's sleep time. I love you."**

**Instead of:** "If you ask one more time, no stories tomorrow!"
**Try:** "We'll read the next chapter tomorrow night. I can't wait."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Keep the routine exactly the same every night. The same book, the same song, the same
  words.
- **4–5:** Offer a "bedtime ticket": one free trip out of bed (for water, a hug, the toilet).
  Once it's used, it's used.
- **6–7:** Let them read quietly to themselves for a set time after lights out. It gives them
  some control, and it helps many children wind down.

**Next time:** Build the connection they're asking for into the routine itself. Five minutes of
one-on-one chat before lights out often cuts down on the stalling that comes after.

---

## Moment 19: "I'm not tired!"

**What's going on:** An overtired child can look wired rather than sleepy. They may be
running around, silly, or cranky, and completely sure they don't need to sleep.

**Say this:**
**"Your body feels full of energy. It doesn't feel like sleepy time to you. Bodies still
need rest, even when they're not tired. You can lie in bed and rest your body quietly.
Do you want the night-light on or off?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Don't argue about whether they're tired. Focus on what they need to do:
**"You don't have to fall asleep. You do need to stay in bed and rest."** Dim the lights, turn
off screens, keep your voice low and slow.

**Instead of:** "You ARE tired. Look at you!"
**Try:** "You don't have to sleep. You just need to rest your body."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Start the bedtime routine earlier. Toddlers who miss their window often get a second
  wind.
- **4–5:** Try a calm-down activity in bed: breathing slowly with a stuffed animal on their tummy,
  watching it rise and fall.
- **6–7:** Explain once, when they're calm, why sleep matters for growing bodies and brains.
  Then let the routine do the work.

**Next time:** Stop screens well before bedtime and dim the lights in the house as bedtime gets
close. A calm last hour makes "I'm not tired" much less common.

---

## Moment 20: "There's a monster in my room"

*Night-time fears*

**What's going on:** Many young children go through phases of being afraid of the dark,
monsters or bad dreams, especially as their imagination grows. The fear is real to them,
even when the monster isn't.

**Say this:**
**"You're scared. Feeling scared at night is really hard. I'm right here, and you're safe.
Do you want the night-light on, or your special bear to guard you?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Stay close for a few minutes and keep your voice calm and low. **"I'll sit
with you until your body feels calmer. You're safe."** Avoid a long search for monsters,
which can make the fear feel more real. A short check and a calm "all clear" is enough.

**Instead of:** "Don't be silly, monsters aren't real."
**Try:** "You feel scared. I'm here, and you're safe."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** A night-light, a favorite stuffed animal, and a short, predictable goodnight routine.
- **4–5:** Give them a "brave tool": a flashlight they can keep by the bed, or a special
  blanket.
- **6–7:** Talk about fears during the day, when they're calm. Draw the monster together and
  give it a silly name. Fears often shrink in daylight.

**Next time:** Keep scary shows and stories away from the hour before bed. If night fears are
very frequent, get worse over time, or come with nightmares that seriously disrupt sleep
for weeks, mention it to your pediatrician (Chapter 10).

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Make a picture routine chart with your child.**

1. Pick one routine to start with: morning or bedtime, whichever is harder in your home.
2. List the steps together, in order. Keep it to five or six steps.
3. Let your child draw a picture for each step, or cut pictures out of magazines, or take
   photos of them doing each step.
4. Stick the chart where the routine happens: by the front door, or next to the bed.
5. From now on, instead of giving orders, ask: **"What's next on the chart?"**

Children follow a routine they helped make much more willingly than one handed to them.
