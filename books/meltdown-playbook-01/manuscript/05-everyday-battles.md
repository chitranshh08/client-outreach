# Part 2: The Playbook: 30 Moments, 30 Scripts

*Don't read this part cover to cover. Find your moment, read the bold line, and go.*

## How to use a script card

Every moment in Part 2 has the same six parts, so you always know where to look.

- **What's going on:** one or two lines on what your child is feeling or needing.
- **Say this:** the script. This is the part to use in the moment.
- **If it gets bigger:** what to say and do if the storm grows.
- **Instead of / Try:** a common phrase that backfires, and a swap.
- **Age tweaks:** small changes for ages 2–3, 4–5 and 6–7.
- **Next time:** one idea to make the moment easier before it starts.

Every script is built on the Calm Words Formula from Chapter 3: Connect, Name it, Hold the
limit, Offer a way out. And every script works best after the 60-Second Reset. Feet,
breathe out, soft face, low voice. Then speak.

If you only have ten seconds, read the **Say this** line and go.

# Chapter 4: "I Don't Want To!": Ending Everyday Battles Without a Fight

Noah is four. It's 5:15 pm at the playground, and his mom has already said "time to go"
three times. Now she's holding his shoes, he's at the top of the slide, and he's yelling
"NO! Five more minutes!"

She's tired. Dinner isn't made. And she knows what comes next: the chase, the carry, the
screaming all the way to the car.

Most meltdowns don't happen during big events. They happen in the small, ordinary moments
of every single day. Leaving, stopping, getting dressed, hearing no, waiting. These
moments come around again and again, which makes them the best place to start. Get
these right and you'll feel the difference by the end of the week.

---

## Moment 1: "Five more minutes!"

*Leaving the park or a playdate*

**What's going on:** Your child is having fun, and leaving means the fun ends. Stopping
something enjoyable is one of the hardest things for a young brain to do, especially
with no warning.

**Say this:**
**"You're having so much fun. It's hard to leave fun things. Two more slides, then shoes.
Do you want to do the last slide fast or slow?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Say the limit once more, calmly. **"It's time to go. I'm going to help
you."** Then help. Carry them if you need to, calmly and without a lecture. Use the 3-Line
Script on the way to the car: **"I see you're sad. I'm right here. I won't let you run away
from me."**

**Instead of:** "If you don't come now, we're never coming back."
**Try:** "You can be sad and still come with me. We'll come back another day."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Use fewer words and more action. "Bye-bye, slide!" and wave together. A snack in
  the car helps a lot.
- **4–5:** Give them a job. "Can you be the one who finds the car?"
- **6–7:** Let them set part of the plan. "Do you want two more slides or one more swing?"

**Next time:** Give a warning before it's time to go, and make it concrete. "Five more
minutes" means little to a young child. "Three more slides, then we go" is something they
can count.

---

## Moment 2: "But I'm not done!"

*Turning off screens*

**What's going on:** Shows and games are designed to be hard to stop. Your child isn't
being difficult on purpose; they're being pulled away from something very absorbing, often
in the middle.

**Say this:**
**"You really wanted to keep watching. It's so hard to stop in the middle. Screen time is
done for today. Do you want to press off, or should I?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Stay calm and follow through. **"I'm going to turn it off now."** Turn it
off, put the device out of sight, and stay close. **"I see you're really mad. I'm right here.
I won't let you throw the tablet."**

**Instead of:** "That's it, no more screens for a week!"
**Try:** "Screen time is done for today. You can have it again tomorrow after lunch."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Stop at the end of a short episode, not in the middle. Say "All done!" and sign it
  with your hands.
- **4–5:** Let them choose where to stop: "at the end of this episode or after this level?"
  Then hold it.
- **6–7:** Agree on the rules together when everyone is calm, and write them down. In the
  moment, point to the rule: "What does our screen rule say?"

**Next time:** Use a visual timer your child can see. It moves the "bad guy" from you to the
timer. "The timer says it's done" is easier to accept than "Mom says."

---

## Moment 3: "I'm not wearing that!"

*Getting dressed*

**What's going on:** Clothes are one of the few things a young child can control, and
sometimes the itchy tag, the wrong color or the tight sleeve really does bother them. Often
it's a mix of wanting control and real discomfort.

**Say this:**
**"You don't like how that shirt feels. You really want to pick. We need to get dressed
before breakfast. Do you want the red shirt or the blue one?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Keep it boring and calm. **"Clothes go on before we go out. I can help,
or you can do it."** If you're running late, it's okay to help them dress while they
protest. Narrate kindly: **"Arm in. Other arm. All done. That was hard."**

**Instead of:** "You're wearing what I picked, end of story."
**Try:** "You can choose between these two."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Offer just two items, holding them up. Make it a game: "Can your arm find the
  sleeve?"
- **4–5:** Let them pick the outfit the night before and lay it out together.
- **6–7:** Give them the rule and let them run it. "It needs to be warm enough for outside.
  You choose the rest."

**Next time:** Choose clothes the night before, when nobody is rushed. If your child
complains about the same textures again and again, take it seriously. Some children feel
tags and seams much more than others.

## Moment 4: "I wanted the BLUE cup!"

*The tiny thing that isn't tiny*

**What's going on:** To you, it's a cup. To your child, it was a plan, and the plan fell
apart. Small disappointments feel huge to young children, especially when they're hungry
or tired.

**Say this:**
**"You wanted the blue cup. That's so disappointing. The blue cup is in the dishwasher.
Do you want the green cup or the yellow one?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Don't argue about whether it's a big deal. It is, to them. Stay close
and let the feeling pass. **"I know. You really wanted blue. I'm right here."** Offer the cup
again once they're calmer.

**Instead of:** "It's just a cup! Stop being silly."
**Try:** "That's disappointing. Blue is your favorite."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Say less. "Blue cup. You wanted blue." Then offer a choice with your hands.
- **4–5:** Use a plan. "Blue cup is for dinner. Let's put it on the counter so it's ready."
- **6–7:** Invite problem-solving. "Blue is dirty. What could we do?" Many six-year-olds come
  up with their own fix, like washing it.

**Next time:** Watch for patterns. If "tiny thing" meltdowns cluster around the same time
of day, check HALT from Chapter 2. A snack before dinner prevents a lot of cup disasters.

## Moment 5: "Why can't I have a cookie?"

*Hearing no*

**What's going on:** Your child wants something, and you said no. This is often a tantrum,
not a meltdown (Chapter 2): they're protesting to change your mind. The feeling is real,
and so is the hope that pushing will work.

**Say this:**
**"You really want a cookie. It's hard to hear no. Cookies are for after dinner. Do you
want an apple or some cheese now?"**

**If it gets bigger:** This is the moment to hold steady. **"I hear you. The answer is still
no. It's okay to be mad about it."** Don't bargain, and don't add new reasons. If you give
in after the screaming starts, the screaming gets louder next time.

**Instead of:** "Fine, just one, if you'll stop crying."
**Try:** "You can be mad. The answer is still no."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Move the cookie out of sight. Out of sight really does help at this age.
- **4–5:** Use "when… then…": "When dinner is finished, then you can have a cookie."
- **6–7:** Give a short reason once, then stop explaining. "Cookies are after dinner because
  dinner is for growing." Repeating the reason turns it into a debate.

**Next time:** Say yes to something whenever you can. "Yes, you can have a cookie after
dinner" lands better than "No cookies now," even though they mean the same thing.

---

## Moment 6: "I can't wait!"

*Waiting in line, waiting for you*

**What's going on:** Waiting is hard work for a young brain. It takes the kind of self-control
that's still being built (Chapter 2). Boredom plus a long line equals trouble.

**Say this:**
**"Waiting is so hard. You're tired of standing here. We have two more people, then it's our
turn. Do you want to hold my hand or hold the basket?"**

**If it gets bigger:** Give their body something to do. **"Let's count the red things we can
see."** **"Can you jump five times, quietly?"** If they're melting down, step out of line if
you can, use the 3-Line Script, and come back when they're calmer.

**Instead of:** "Stop whining, we'll be done soon."
**Try:** "Waiting is hard. Let's play a game while we wait."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Keep waits short when you can, and bring something to hold or chew.
- **4–5:** Play "I spy," count things, or make up a story together.
- **6–7:** Give them a real job: holding the list, finding the next item, counting the change.

**Next time:** Plan for waiting before it happens. Bring a small "waiting kit": a snack, a
tiny toy, a book. And when waiting at home ("Just a minute!"), make it concrete: "When I
finish this email, I'll come play."

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Pick two daily transitions and use a warning plus a script for five days.**

1. Choose the two moments from this chapter that happen most in your home. (Leaving
   somewhere and screens off are the most common.)
2. Before each one, give a concrete warning: "Three more slides." "At the end of this
   episode."
3. When the moment comes, use the **Say this** line.
4. At the end of five days, notice what changed. Even one shorter meltdown is progress.

Transitions get easier with repetition. The more predictable your words are, the less
your child has to fight them.
