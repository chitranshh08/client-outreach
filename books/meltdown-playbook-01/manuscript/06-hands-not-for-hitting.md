# Chapter 5: Hands Are Not for Hitting: Stopping Hitting, Biting and Throwing Calmly

Lily is three. Her mom said no to a second popsicle, and before anyone could blink, Lily
smacked her mom's arm. Hard.

Her mom's first feeling wasn't calm. It was shock, then a flash of anger, then a wave of
worry. *Is my child aggressive? Did I do something wrong? If I don't come down hard now,
will she hit other kids at daycare?*

If your child hits, bites or throws things, you are not alone, and your child is not a bad
kid. Young children have strong feelings, quick hands, and not many words yet. When a
feeling gets too big, it often comes out through the body before it can come out through
the mouth.

Your job isn't to punish that feeling away. It's to do two things, in this order:

1. **Stop the hurting, calmly and right away.**
2. **Later, teach a safer way to let the mad out.**

## Block first, talk later

With hitting, biting and throwing, **safety always comes before words.** Don't stand three
feet away saying "We don't hit" while the hitting continues. Move first.

- **Block.** Catch the hand, move your arm, step back, or gently hold their wrists.
- **Move.** Put space between your child and whoever they're hurting. Pick them up if you
  need to.
- **Say the limit once.** Short and calm. **"I won't let you hit."**

Then do the 60-Second Reset and use the rest of the script. The big conversation about
kind hands comes later, when everyone is calm (the after-the-storm talk from Chapter 3).

A quiet, firm, *boring* response works better than a big reaction. Some children hit
partly because it gets a huge reaction. When hitting gets the same calm block every time,
it stops being interesting.

## The "safe mad" list

Telling a child "don't hit" leaves a gap: *then what do I do with this mad feeling?* The
answer is a **safe mad list**: a short list of things your child *is* allowed to do when
they're angry. Mad is okay. Hurting is not.

Good things for a safe mad list:

- Stomp your feet hard
- Squeeze a pillow or a stress ball
- Roar like a lion, or yell into a pillow
- Push against a wall with both hands
- Rip up scrap paper
- Scribble hard on a "mad page"
- Ask for a tight hug

You'll build your own family list at the end of this chapter. Once you have it, the scripts
below end with an invitation to use it.

## Moment 7: When they hit you

**What's going on:** Your child is flooded with a feeling (often anger or frustration), and it
came out through their hands. Hitting a parent can happen because you're the person they
feel safest falling apart with.

**Say this:**
*(Block the hand first.)*
**"I won't let you hit me. You're really mad. You can stomp your feet or squeeze this
pillow. I'm right here."**

**If it gets bigger:** Keep blocking, and move out of reach if you need to. **"I'm going to step
back so I don't get hurt. I'm still right here."** Stay close enough to be there, far enough
to be safe. Once the storm passes, reconnect: **"That was a big mad. You're okay. I'm okay."**

**Instead of:** Hitting back, or "How do YOU like it?"
**Try:** "I won't let you hurt me, and I won't hurt you."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Use very few words: "No hitting. Stomp!" and show them how to stomp.
- **4–5:** Name the feeling and the swap: "You're so mad. Show me mad with your feet."
- **6–7:** Afterward, talk about repair. "When you hit me, it hurt. What could you do to help
  fix it?" A hug, a drawing, or a sorry works. Don't force the word "sorry" during the storm.

**Next time:** Look for the build-up. Many children tense up, get louder or clench their fists
before they hit. When you see the signs, step in early: "I can see you're getting mad.
Want to stomp it out?"

## Moment 8: When they hit another child

**What's going on:** Another child took a toy, got in their space, or said something that
stung. Your child didn't have the words or the patience, so their hand did the talking.

**Say this:**
*(Separate the children first. Then go to the child who got hurt.)*
To the hurt child: **"Are you okay? That hurt. I'm here."**
To your child: **"I won't let you hit. You were so mad he took the truck. Let's use words:
'Stop! That's mine!'"**

**If it gets bigger:** Take your child a few steps away to calm down, with you, not alone.
**"Let's take a break over here until your body is calm. Then we'll go back."** Stay with them
and use the 3-Line Script.

**Instead of:** "Say sorry right now!"
**Try:** "Let's check if Sam is okay." Once your child is calm, help them repair: bringing Sam
a toy or asking "Are you okay?" teaches more than a forced "sorry."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Stay very close during play so you can block early. Give them simple words to
  use: "Stop!" "Mine!" "Help!"
- **4–5:** Practice the words at home when everyone is calm. Role-play with stuffed animals:
  "Bear took Bunny's ball. What can Bunny say?"
- **6–7:** Talk through what happened afterward. "What did you want? What could you do next
  time?" Help them come up with their own plan.

**Next time:** Shorter playdates, more snacks, and staying close to children who are just
learning to share. Many hitting moments happen when children are tired or have been
together a long time.

## Moment 9: When they bite

**What's going on:** Biting is common in toddlers and usually fades as children learn more
words.[^S6] A child might bite out of frustration, excitement, teething, or to see what
happens. It is upsetting, but in young toddlers it's a normal stage, not a sign of a mean
child.

**Say this:**
*(Gently move your child away. Check the person who was bitten first.)*
**"I won't let you bite. Biting hurts. You're so frustrated. You can bite this teether (or
this apple). You can say 'Stop!'"**

**If it gets bigger:** Keep them close and calm. **"Teeth are for food. I'll keep everyone safe."**
Give their mouth something safe to do: a chewy snack, a cold teether, a drink of water.

**Instead of:** Biting them back "so they know how it feels."
**Try:** "Biting hurts. I won't let you do it. Here's something you can bite."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Keep it short and very calm. Big reactions can make biting *more* interesting. Offer
  something safe to chew.
- **4–5:** Biting usually fades as words grow, so at this age look closely for the trigger
  (Chapter 2's HALT) and practice words for that exact situation.
- **6–7:** Treat it like hitting. If biting keeps happening at this age, it's worth talking
  to your pediatrician (Chapter 10).

**Next time:** Notice when biting happens. Is it at daycare pickup? When tired? During
rough play? Many parents find one clear pattern, and changing that situation stops most
bites.

## Moment 10: When toys start flying

*Throwing things in anger*

**What's going on:** Throwing is a fast, satisfying way to get a feeling out of the body. The
trouble is that it breaks things and can hurt people.

**Say this:**
**"I won't let you throw the blocks. You're really angry. You can throw these soft socks
into the basket, or stomp with me."**

**If it gets bigger:** Move the throwable things out of reach, calmly. **"I'm going to put the
blocks away until your body is calmer."** It's not a punishment; it's keeping everyone safe.
Then use the 3-Line Script and stay close.

**Instead of:** "Pick that up RIGHT NOW!"
**Try:** "I'll keep the blocks safe for now. When you're calm, we'll clean up together."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Throwing is also how toddlers explore. Offer a "yes" to throwing: soft balls or
  scarves into a basket. "Balls are for throwing. Blocks are for building."
- **4–5:** Clean up together once calm. It's not a punishment; it's showing them how to fix
  what happened.
- **6–7:** Give them the repair job: "The tower got knocked over. What's your plan to fix it?"

**Next time:** Keep a "throw basket" with a few soft things in it. When you see the mad
building, point to it: "Need to throw? Use the throw basket."

## When to get more help

Hitting, biting and throwing are common in young children. But talk to your pediatrician
if the behavior is very frequent, is getting worse instead of better over several weeks,
is causing real injuries, or seems to come with no trigger at all. Chapter 10 covers this
in more detail. Asking for help early is a smart move, not a failure.

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Make your family's "safe mad" list, together, when everyone is calm.**

1. Pick a quiet time. Say: "Everybody gets mad sometimes. Let's make a list of safe things
   to do when we're mad."
2. Let your child choose three to five ideas from the list in this chapter, or invent their
   own.
3. Write or draw them on a piece of paper and stick it on the fridge.
4. Practice once, for fun: "Show me your best mad stomp!"
5. Next time a hit, bite or throw is coming, point to the list: "What's on your safe mad
   list?"
