# Chapter 3: Four Sentences That Calm Almost Any Storm

Ava is six. Her best friend is going home after a playdate, and Ava is not ready. She
grabs the friend's backpack and won't let go. "You can't leave! It's not fair!"

Her mom has two voices in her head. One says, *Be firm. She can't grab things.* The other
says, *Be kind. She's just sad.* Most parents feel pulled between those two voices all the
time, and they end up flipping between them. Too strict, then too soft, then strict
again.

Here's the secret: **you don't have to choose.** The best responses are kind *and* firm at
the same time. This chapter gives you a simple formula that does both, in four sentences.

## The Calm Words Formula

The 3-Line Meltdown Script from Chapter 1 is your emergency tool. It's what you say when
your mind is blank. The **Calm Words Formula** is the full version. Once you know it, you
can build your own script for any situation, including ones this book never mentions.

It has four steps:

1. **Connect:** show your child you understand what they wanted.
2. **Name it:** put a word to the feeling.
3. **Hold the limit:** say calmly what will and won't happen.
4. **Offer a way out:** give them something they *can* do.

Here's Ava's mom using all four:

**Connect:** "You really wanted Zoe to stay longer."
**Name it:** "You're so sad the playdate is over."
**Hold the limit:** "I won't let you take her backpack. Zoe is going home now."
**Offer a way out:** "Do you want to walk her to the door, or wave from the window?"

Four sentences. Kind the whole way through, and the backpack still goes home with Zoe.

Let's look at why each step matters.

## Step 1: Connect

Connecting means showing your child that you understand what they *wanted*, before you
say anything about what they did.

**"You really wanted Zoe to stay longer."**
**"You wanted to keep playing."**
**"You were hoping for the red one."**

You're not agreeing that they should get it. You're just showing that you see the world
from where they're standing. For a child in a storm, feeling understood is often the first
thing that helps their body relax.

*Tip:* start with "You wanted…" or "You were hoping…" It's almost impossible to get
wrong, and it keeps you out of arguing about whether the want was reasonable.

## Step 2: Name It

Naming means putting a simple word to the feeling.

**"You're so disappointed."**
**"You're really frustrated."**
**"That made you mad."**

This step does more than it seems. Siegel and Bryson call it "name it to tame it."[^S5]
There's research behind the idea, too. In one brain-imaging study, adults who put their
feelings into words showed less activity in the amygdala, a part of the brain that reacts
strongly to threats.[^S4] That study was done with adults, not children, so we can't claim
the same result for a four-year-old. But it fits what many parents notice: a feeling that
has a name often feels a little less huge.

Naming also teaches. Every time you say "You're frustrated," your child learns a word
they'll eventually use themselves, instead of using their fists or feet.

*Tips for naming:*

- **Keep it simple.** Mad, sad, scared, frustrated, disappointed, worried, excited. You
  don't need fancy words.
- **Guess, don't announce.** If you're not sure, say "I wonder if you're feeling…" If you
  guess wrong, your child will often correct you. That's still a win: they just named
  their own feeling.
- **Match the size.** A huge storm deserves a strong word. "You're SO mad" lands better
  than a flat "You seem upset."

## Step 3: Hold the Limit

This is the firm part. You say calmly what will and won't happen.

**"I won't let you take her backpack."**
**"The show is finished for today."**
**"We're leaving the park now."**

Notice three things about these sentences.

**They're about what you will do.** "I won't let you hit" works better than "Don't hit!"
because it doesn't depend on your child choosing to obey in a moment when they can't. It
tells them the adult will keep everyone safe.

**They're calm and short.** No lecture, no threat, no "how many times have I told you."
Say it once, in a low voice, like a fact about the weather.

**They don't change because of the storm.** If screaming changes the limit, your child
learns that screaming works, and you'll hear more of it. Kind but firm means the feeling
is welcome and the limit still stands.

*Not every moment needs a limit.* If your child is sad and not doing anything unsafe or
off-limits, skip step 3. Connect, name it, and offer comfort. That's enough.

## Step 4: Offer a Way Out

A child in a meltdown is stuck. They want something they can't have, and they don't know
what to do next. The last step hands them a door out.

There are three kinds of way out. Pick whichever fits.

**A choice.** Two options you're happy with.
**"Do you want to walk to the car or ride on my back?"**
**"Do you want to turn off the TV, or should I?"**

**Comfort.** Something to help their body settle.
**"Do you want a hug, or do you want me to sit next to you?"**
**"Let's take a big dragon breath together."**

**A plan.** Something to look forward to.
**"Let's write 'park' on the calendar for Saturday."**
**"You can have the red one next time."**

Choices work because they give your child a little bit of control back, and a child who
feels in control has less reason to fight. Keep choices small, keep them to two, and only
offer ones you're happy with either way.

## Putting it together: a worked example

Let's build a script from scratch for a moment that happens in lots of homes: **turning off
the tablet.**

*The situation:* Your five-year-old has been playing a game on the tablet. You said "five
more minutes" five minutes ago. Now you're taking it, and he's clinging to it and yelling.

**Start with the reset.** Feet planted, one slow breath out, soft face, low voice.

**Connect.** What did he want? To keep playing. **"You really wanted to finish your game."**

**Name it.** What's the feeling? Probably frustration. **"It's so frustrating to stop in the
middle."**

**Hold the limit.** What's non-negotiable? The tablet time is over. **"Tablet time is done
for today. I'm going to put it on the shelf."**

**Offer a way out.** A choice works well here. **"Do you want to press the off button, or
should I?"**

Here's the full script, all four steps together:

> **"You really wanted to finish your game. It's so frustrating to stop in the middle.
> Tablet time is done for today. Do you want to press the off button, or should I?"**

That's about fifteen seconds of talking. Then, just like with the 3-Line Script, **stay
close and stay quiet.** If he keeps yelling, you can calmly repeat the limit and the
choice, word for word. Repeating the same calm words is more effective than finding new
ones each time.

## Why the usual phrases backfire

You may be wondering why this book avoids some phrases almost every parent uses. Here's
the short version.

**"Calm down!"** A child who could calm down on command would already be calm. Hearing
it can feel like being told off for having a feeling. *Instead, try:* the reset, then
**"I'm right here. Let's breathe together."**

**"Stop crying."** Crying is how young children release a big feeling. Asking them to stop
often makes the feeling come out another way. *Instead, try:* **"It's okay to cry. I'm
staying with you."**

**"One… two… three…"** Counting can get quick results, but it doesn't teach anything about
feelings, and many children learn to wait for "two and a half." *Instead, try:* hold the
limit and offer a choice.

**Bribes ("If you stop crying, you can have a lollipop").** A bribe in the moment teaches
that meltdowns can lead to treats. *Instead, try:* offer comfort now, and plan a treat for
a calm moment later if you want to.

**"You're fine."** When a child is clearly not fine, this teaches them that their feelings
don't count. *Instead, try:* name what you see. **"That really hurt your feelings."**

None of these phrases makes you a bad parent. They're what almost everyone says when
they're tired. The point is to have something better ready.

## After the storm: the talk that teaches

The Calm Words Formula gets you *through* the moment. But the real learning happens
*afterward*, once your child's upstairs brain is back online.

Wait until everyone is calm. That might be ten minutes later, or at bedtime, or the next
morning. Then have a short, relaxed chat. Three questions are enough:

**"What happened?"** Let them tell it their way. Listen more than you talk.

**"How did you feel?"** Help them find the word if they're stuck. **"Were you frustrated?
Disappointed?"**

**"What could we do next time?"** Make a plan together. **"Next time the game has to end,
maybe we can set a timer so you know it's coming."**

Keep it short, warm and forward-looking. This isn't the time for punishment or a replay
of everything they did wrong. It's practice for the next storm.

## When you don't remember the formula

Some days, you won't remember four steps. That's what the 3-Line Script is for. If your
mind goes blank, fall back to it:

**"I see you're ___. I'm right here. I won't let you ___."**

The 3-Line Script is really the Calm Words Formula in its shortest form. "I see you're"
covers Connect and Name it. "I won't let you" covers Hold the limit. And "I'm right here"
is the comfort that helps your child find the way out.
