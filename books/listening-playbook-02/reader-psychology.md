# Reader Psychology: The Parent Who Isn't Being Listened To

_Written 2026-10-03 before drafting. It sets the tone, structure and hooks for the
manuscript, the listing and the ads. Facts marked [S#] are in `sources.md`. Parent phrases
are paraphrased patterns from parenting forums and articles, not quotes from real people.
They're for our understanding and must not appear in the book as testimonials._

## 1. The loop she's stuck in

```
Ask from across the room ──► no response ──► ask again (louder, more words)
        ▲                                              │
        │                                              ▼
 next request: she                    3rd, 4th, 5th ask: irritation builds
 "knows" it'll take 5                              │
        │                                              ▼
 relief, then guilt ◄── child moves ◄── she YELLS and finally gets up
```

What each side learns from one loop:
- **The child learns** that the first four asks mean nothing. The real ask is the one where
  the voice changes and the parent moves. Nothing happened after asks 1–4, so ignoring
  them made sense.
- **The parent learns** that yelling works, because the child moved. It didn't. The
  follow-through worked: on the fifth ask she finally walked over, and the yell happened
  to come with it. She never sees that difference, so she keeps yelling and hating it.

**This is the book's central insight.** It must land in Chapter 2: *keep the follow-through,
drop the yell.* It takes the shame off ("you're not a yeller, you're a late follow-througher")
and gives her a lever she can pull tomorrow.

## 3. The expectation gap (why she's angrier than the situation deserves)

- 56% of parents believe children can resist doing something forbidden **before age 3**;
  36% believe it **before age 2**. Brain research says this skill only *starts* developing
  around 3.5–4 years [S6].
- Self-control skills keep developing for years, into adolescence and early adulthood [S1].

**So** a lot of "he's ignoring me on purpose" is really "he can't switch his attention
off the train set yet." When a parent sees this, the anger drops a notch, and a calmer
parent follows through better. Chapter 2 delivers this gently: it's *information*, not
a correction.

## 4. Why the child doesn't listen (the mechanics we fix)

| Reason | Fix in the book | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Asked from across the room | Get close | [S2] [S3] |
| No eye contact, mid-play | Get their eyes first | [S2] |
| Asked as a question ("Can you…?") | Tell, kindly: "Shoes on, please." | [S2] [S3] |
| Told what *not* to do ("Stop jumping!") | Say what to do: "Feet on the floor." | [S2] [S7] |
| Several instructions at once | One at a time | [S2] [S3] |

| Nothing happens after asks 1–4 | Follow through, every time | [S2] |
| Yelling and lectures give attention to the wrong thing | Notice and name the behavior you *want* | [S7] |
| Consequence is huge, late or unrelated ("no TV for a week!") | Related, small, soon; one warning, then calm follow-through | [S2] [S8] [S4] |

## 5. Her objections (answer each in the text, without arguing)

1. **"I've tried everything."** She has tried *pieces* (counting, charts, threats) without a
   full sequence. The book's promise is a complete sequence, small enough to remember.
2. **"Gentle parenting is permissive."** Firmness is built in: the ask is said once and the
   follow-through always happens. Kind tone, firm action.
3. **"My kid is strong-willed."** Every card has *If they still don't* and age tweaks. Testing
   is expected, not a sign the method failed.
4. **"I don't have time to walk over every time."** Walking over once is faster than five
   shouts and a standoff. Make this point with a time comparison in Chapter 1.
5. **"Consequences feel mean."** Related, small, soon, said calmly. The consequence teaches;
   your tone keeps it from hurting.
6. **"But bribes work."** Rewards planned *before* are fine; offers made *during* a standoff
   teach kids to hold out. Chapter 3 makes the line clear.

## 7. Language rules for the manuscript (from this analysis)

- Name her pain in her own words in the first lines of every chapter ("You've asked
  four times").
- Never call the child defiant, manipulative, bad or a brat. Use "testing", "busy",
  "not ready to stop".
- Never imply she damaged her child. Facts about yelling are framed as "it doesn't work
  well long-term" [S4], not "it harms your child".
- Promise **fewer** repeats, never "listen the first time, every time".
- Give her permission: to skip a battle, to not be perfect, to walk away from a standoff.
- One idea per paragraph; short lines; bold for anything she'd say out loud.
