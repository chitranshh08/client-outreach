# Chapter 10: When They Still Won't Listen

Ezra is four. For a week, his mom has used the One Clear Ask every morning. She walks
over, gets his eyes, says "Shoes on, please," and waits. Some mornings it works on the
first try. But this morning, Ezra looked right at her, said "No," and lay down on the
floor.

*It's not working,* she thinks. *Maybe this method is fine for other kids. Not mine.*

If you're feeling that way, this chapter is for you. Before you decide something isn't
working, let's check how you're measuring it, what might be getting in the way, and when
it's time to ask for more help.

## Measure progress the right way

No method gets a child to listen every time. If that's the test, everything fails. Here's
what progress really looks like:

- **Fewer repeats.** You used to ask five times. Now it's usually once or twice.
- **Faster starts.** Your child begins moving sooner after you ask.
- **Less climbing.** You get to Rung 3 of the ladder less often than you did.
- **A calmer you.** You yell less, even on hard days.

Get out the tally you started in Chapter 2. Do one more hour of counting now, and compare.
Progress often shows up in the numbers before it shows up in your feelings.

## Expect it to get worse before it gets better

When you change how you respond, many children push harder at first to see whether the
change is real. The CDC warns parents about this when they start ignoring whining: the
behavior may get worse at first.[^S11] The same can happen with a new follow-through.

This is a good sign, not a bad one. It means your child noticed the change. Keep going,
with the same words, for at least a week before you judge.

## Check your asks

Sometimes the method slips without us noticing. Run through these five questions:

1. **Am I still asking from far away?** Walking over is the step most of us drop first.
2. **Am I asking questions?** "Can you…?" and "…okay?" creep back in.
3. **Am I giving too many instructions?** If your tally showed a lot of marks, try cutting
   the number of asks in half. Let the small stuff go.
4. **Am I waiting?** Or am I repeating after two seconds?
5. **Am I following through every time?** If the follow-through happens only some of the
   time, the first ask will only work some of the time.

Pick the one that sounds most like you this week, and fix just that one.

## Are the grown-ups saying the same thing?

Child psychiatrists call consistency and predictability the cornerstones of discipline.[^S8]
That's hard when two or three adults are asking in different ways.

You don't need to agree on everything. Start small:

- **Three shared phrases.** For example: "Shoes on, please." "Rule check." "When… then…"
- **One shared consequence** for the moment that's hardest in your house.
- **Back each other up.** If one adult asks, the other doesn't take over or undo it. They
  can say, **"Mom asked. I'll wait with you."**

For grandparents and sitters, give them the pocket cards from Chapters 1 and 3. A one-page
card is easier to share than a whole philosophy.

## Check underneath

If your child is suddenly much harder to reach, look at what's going on underneath. If
you've read *The Meltdown Playbook*, you'll remember **HALT**: is your child Hungry,
Angry, Lonely or Tired? Is there a big change going on, like a new baby, a move or a new
school? Children who are running on empty have less to give to listening. Sometimes the
fix is a snack, an earlier bedtime or ten minutes of your full attention, not a better
script.

## When "won't" turns into "can't"

Sometimes refusing turns into falling apart: screaming, crying, kicking. At that point
your child can't listen, however clear your ask is. Switch to feelings first, help them
calm down, and come back to the task afterward. That's what *The Meltdown Playbook* is
for.

## When to talk to your pediatrician

Many listening struggles are a normal part of growing up. But sometimes they're a sign
that something else needs attention. Please talk to your child's doctor if:

- **Your child often doesn't follow directions, even simple ones, even when you're close and
  they're looking at you.** The CDC notes that this can be mistaken for not paying attention
  or ignoring, when it may actually be a hearing problem. If you think your child might have
  hearing loss, ask their doctor for a hearing check as soon as possible.[^S14]
- **Listening is hard for your child everywhere,** at home, at daycare and at school, and
  teachers or caregivers have raised concerns too.
- **Your child has lost skills they used to have,** like words, or following simple routines
  they used to follow.[^S15]
- **Anyone is getting hurt,** or the behavior feels unsafe.
- **You're worried.** You know your child best. You never need a reason beyond that to ask.

Bring your tally and a few examples. Concrete notes make the conversation with your doctor
much more useful.

This book can't diagnose anything, and nothing here replaces a conversation with someone
who knows your child.

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Notice which rung made the biggest difference.** Think back over the past week. Was it
getting close? The reminder with a choice? Helping hands? "When… then…"? Write it down.
That's your strongest tool, and it's the one to reach for first on a hard day.
