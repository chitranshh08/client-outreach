# Chapter 7: Table Talk: Mealtimes Without the Battle

Arlo is three. Dinner is on the table, and Arlo is under it. When his dad gets him into
his booster seat, he slides out again. When he finally stays, he looks at his plate and
announces, "Yuck. I'm not eating that."

Mealtimes pack a lot of asks into a short time: come to the table, sit down, stay
sitting, eat, don't throw, say please. No wonder they turn into battles.

This chapter is about **behavior at the table**, not nutrition. For what and how much
children should eat, your pediatrician is the right person to ask. But one idea from
feeding experts makes every moment in this chapter easier, so let's start there.

## Your job and their job

Dietitian and family therapist Ellyn Satter describes a **division of responsibility** in
feeding. For toddlers and older children, the parent decides *what* food is served, *when*
and *where*. The child decides *how much* to eat, and *whether* to eat at all.[^S10]

This takes a huge weight off mealtimes. It means you don't have to win the "three more
bites" battle. Your job is to put the food on the table, at the table, at mealtime, and to
keep the table pleasant. Your child's job is to decide how much of it goes in. That's a
division you can hold calmly, and it leaves very little to fight about.

## Moment 18: Won't come to the table

*"Dinner's ready!" and nobody comes*

**What's going on:** Dinner means stopping play, and young children don't feel hungry on
a schedule. "Dinner's ready!" from the kitchen is easy to ignore.

**Say this:**
[Five minutes before:] **"Five minutes until dinner. Finish your puzzle piece."**
[At dinner time, walk over:] **"Dinner's on the table. Come sit with us, please."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Dinner time. Do you want to carry the napkins or the spoons?"**
- *Rung 2:* **"Let's walk together."** [Take their hand.]
- *Rung 3:* **"We're starting. Your plate is ready when you are."** Start eating without
  chasing them. Dinner is at dinner time. There's no separate meal later, and the next food
  comes at the next planned snack or meal.[^S10]

**Instead of:** "Get here NOW or there's no dinner!"
**Try:** **"Dinner's on the table. We'd love you to join us."** [And start eating.]

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Carry them to the table with a song. Toddlers often come happily once they're
  there.
- **4–5:** Give them a job that starts at the table: pouring water, handing out napkins.
- **6–7:** Let them help make one part of the meal, so dinner is partly theirs.

**Next time:** Give the five-minute warning every night, and keep dinner around the same
time. A predictable dinner is easier to come to.

## Moment 19: Won't stay in the seat

*Up, down, under the table, back again*

**What's going on:** Young children can only sit still for a short time, and a long family
dinner is often longer than they can manage. Getting up is often their body saying "I'm
done."

**Say this:**
**"Bottom on the chair, please."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Bottom on the chair. Are you still eating, or are you done?"**
- *Rung 2:* **"Leaving the table means you're done eating."** If they leave, calmly clear their
  plate. Remember, how much to eat is their decision.[^S10]
- *Rung 4:* No lecture. **"Okay. You can play quietly while we finish."**

**Instead of:** "Sit DOWN! How many times do I have to tell you?"
**Try:** **"Are you done, or still eating?"** Let the answer decide.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Expect a short meal. A booster or high chair helps, and it's okay if they're
  done before you are.
- **4–5:** Teach a polite way to leave: **"May I be excused?"**
- **6–7:** Ask them to stay at the table a little after eating, for a bit of family talk.
  Keep it short and pleasant.

**Next time:** Serve their plate when they sit down, not five minutes before. Less waiting
means less wandering.

## Moment 20: "Yuck! I'm not eating that!"

*New or disliked food*

**What's going on:** Many young children are cautious about new foods. "Yuck" is often just
"I don't know this one." The more pressure they feel to eat it, the more it becomes a
battle.

**Say this:**
**"You don't have to eat it. You can leave it on the side of your plate."**
[And for the word "yuck":] **"In our family we say 'No, thank you.'"**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Try again with 'No, thank you.'"**
- *Rung 2:* If they keep saying "yuck" to get a reaction, don't give one. Turn the talk to
  something else. Minor behaviors like this often fade when they stop getting attention.[^S11]

**Instead of:** "Just three more bites, and then you can get down."
**Try:** **"You don't have to eat it. Is your tummy still hungry, or all full?"**

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Put a tiny amount of the new food next to something they usually eat. No
  comments, no pressure.
- **4–5:** Let them describe it instead of eating it: **"Is it crunchy or soft?"**
- **6–7:** Invite them to help choose or cook one new food each week.

**Next time:** Serve at least one food your child usually eats at every meal, alongside
whatever else is on the table. They'll always have something to eat, and you won't need to
make a separate meal.

*If you're worried about your child's growth, weight, or a very short list of foods they'll
eat, talk to your pediatrician.*

## Moment 21: Playing with or dropping food

*Food art, food throwing, food on the floor*

**What's going on:** Sometimes it's experimenting: what happens if I squish this? Often,
especially with toddlers, it's a sign they're full.

**Say this:**
**"Food stays on the plate. Are you done, or still eating?"**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Spoon to your mouth, please. Or are you all done?"**
- *Rung 3:* **"Dropping food tells me you're done."** [Calmly take the plate away.]
- *Rung 4:* **"Okay, all done. Let's wipe your hands."** No scolding.

**Instead of:** "Stop playing with your food!"
**Try:** **"Food is for eating. Show me how you eat it."**

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Some mess is normal. Give them finger foods and a way to say "all done," like a
  hand sign.
- **4–5:** Say the rule once, then follow through by ending the meal.
- **6–7:** If they drop food on purpose, they help clean it up: **"Let's wipe it together."**

**Next time:** Smaller portions. A child can always ask for more. A big plate is an
invitation to play.

## Moment 22: Forgetting please and thank you

*"Give me the milk!"*

**What's going on:** Manners are habits, and habits take a long time to build. Young children
forget, especially when they're hungry or excited. It's rarely rudeness.

**Say this:**
[Quietly, without a lecture:] **"Try that again with 'please.'"**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"I'll pass the milk when I hear 'please.'"** [Then wait, calmly.]
- *Rung 4:* As soon as they say it, pass it with a smile. **"Thank you for asking nicely."**

**Instead of:** "What do you SAY?" (Especially in front of other people.)
**Try:** Model it, every time. **"Please pass the potatoes." "Thank you!"** Children pick up a lot
from what they hear you say every day.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Say it for them and let them copy: **"Milk, please."** Then pass it.
- **4–5:** A quiet reminder: **"Magic words?"** is fine, as long as it's warm.
- **6–7:** Notice it out loud when they remember on their own: **"You said thank you to Grandma
  all by yourself."**

**Next time:** Make manners part of the routine, not a test. Say please and thank you to
your child, too. They'll hear it many times a day.

---

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Plan one family meal with a two-rule table plan.** Choose two simple rules, like "bottoms
on chairs" and "food stays on the plate," and tell everyone before the meal starts. Follow
through on both, calmly, and notice out loud when your child keeps them.
