# Chapter 5: "You're Not the Boss of Me!": No, Defiance and Power Struggles

Jonah is five. His dad asks him to turn off the tablet and brush his teeth. Jonah crosses
his arms, looks his dad right in the eye and says, "No. You can't make me."

His dad feels it land like a slap. *Did my five-year-old just challenge me?* The urge to
win is strong. Louder voice, bigger threat, whatever it takes.

But here's the thing about power struggles: it takes two people to have one. The moments
in this chapter go best when you don't pick up the rope. You don't need to win the
argument. You just need to follow through on the ask, calmly, while your child has their
feelings about it.

Testing limits is a normal part of growing up. It isn't a sign that your child is bad or
that you've lost control. It's your child checking whether the rules are real. Each calm
follow-through answers the question: *yes, they are.*

---

## Moment 7: "No!"

*The flat refusal*

**What's going on:** "No" is one of the first ways a young child feels powerful. Often it
comes out automatically, before they've even thought about the ask.

**Say this:**
[Don't argue with the "no." Restate the ask as a calm fact, with a choice.]
**"Coat on. Do you want to zip it yourself, or should I?"**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 2:* **"I'll help you."** [Hold the coat and guide their arms in. Calm hands, no fuss.]
- *Rung 4:* **"Coat's on. Let's go see the ducks."**

**Instead of:** "Don't you say no to me!"
**Try:** Let the word go by. Answer the ask, not the "no." **"Coat on. I'll help."**

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Fewer words, more doing. **"Coat on,"** then gently help. Many toddlers say no and
  then cooperate once they're moving.
- **4–5:** Give them a bigger choice: **"Coat or sweater?"** Either one works for you.
- **6–7:** Acknowledge the no once: **"You don't want to. I get it. Coat on, please."**

**Next time:** Ask fewer yes-or-no questions, and give your child plenty of safe chances to
say no where it doesn't matter. **"Do you want peas? No? Okay!"** A child who gets to say no
sometimes has less need to say it all the time.

---

## Moment 8: "You can't make me!"

*The power challenge*

**What's going on:** Your child is testing who's in charge. And in a way, they're right:
you can't make them *want* to brush their teeth. But you're in charge of what happens next.

**Say this:**
**"You're right. I can't make you want to. Teeth now, please, and then we'll read."**
[Then stop talking and wait.]

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 2:* **"When your teeth are brushed, then we'll read the story."** [Wait in the
  bathroom doorway.]
- *Rung 3:* **"We used up story time, so tonight it's one short song instead. We'll try
  again tomorrow."** [Brush teeth together, calmly, then sing the song.] The time used up is
  the story, not your closeness. Your child still gets you at bedtime.
- *Rung 4:* **"Good night. I love you."** No cold shoulder.

**Instead of:** "Oh, yes I can!" (Now you're in a contest, and you both want to win.)
**Try:** **"I can't make you. I can tell you what happens next."**

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Rarely said in these words. If it is, skip the talk and use helping hands.
- **4–5:** Keep it short. The fewer words you use, the less there is to argue with.
- **6–7:** Later, when calm, ask: **"What would make teeth time easier?"** They may have a
  good idea.

**Next time:** Give your child real control in other places: picking the pajamas, choosing
the book, deciding which tooth to brush first. A child who feels in charge of some things
fights less over the rest.

---

## Moment 9: Running off when you call

*Including the safety version*

**What's going on:** For many young children, running away when called is a great game.
You chase, they laugh. They don't yet understand danger the way adults do.

**First, the safety rule.** Near streets, parking lots, water or crowds, **skip the ladder.**
Hold hands *before* the danger starts. If your child runs, catch them and carry them. Skip
the warnings and the choices, and talk about it afterward.

**Say this (before getting out of the car):**
**"Parking lot rule: hands hold my hand or the car. Which one?"**

**Say this (in a safe, fenced place):**
[Don't chase. Stop, crouch down and call once.]
**"Freeze! Come back to me, please."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 2:* Walk to them calmly and take their hand. **"We're walking together now."**
- *Rung 3:* **"You ran away from me, so you'll ride in the stroller (or hold my hand) the
  rest of the way."**

**Instead of:** Chasing them, laughing or yelling. (It makes the game more fun.)
**Try:** In safe places, don't play the chase. In unsafe places, don't give them the chance:
hold hands first.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Use the stroller, a cart or a carrier near danger. Toddlers can't be trusted to
  stop, however well they know the rule.
- **4–5:** Practice "Freeze!" at home as a game, and give big praise for freezing.
- **6–7:** Explain the danger once, simply: **"Drivers can't see small people between
  cars."**

**Next time:** Play the Freeze game often when nothing is at stake. Call "Freeze!", they
stop, you cheer. Practice now makes the real thing more likely to work.

## Moment 10: Doing it while looking right at you

*Testing the rule*

**What's going on:** Your child is running an experiment: *What happens if I do it again?*
If the answer is a big reaction, that's interesting and worth repeating. Psychologist
Alan Kazdin points out that attention to unwanted behavior tends to increase it, while
attention to the behavior you want increases that instead.[^S7]

**Say this:**
[Calm, boring voice. Same words every time.]
**"Cup stays on the table. If it drops again, the cup goes away."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 3:* [Quietly take the cup away.] **"The cup dropped, so it's going away for now."**
- *Rung 4:* Move on. A few minutes later, offer it back: **"Want to try again? Cup stays on
  the table."**

**Instead of:** A big reaction: gasping, raised voice, a long speech.
**Try:** The most boring follow-through you can manage. Save your excitement for the good
moments: **"You kept your cup on the table the whole meal!"**

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Toddlers drop things to learn how the world works, too. Remove the item without
  any fuss, and give them something they *can* drop, like a ball.
- **4–5:** Say the rule and the consequence once, then act.
- **6–7:** If it's a pattern, talk later: **"I noticed you kept doing it while you looked at
  me. What was going on?"**

**Next time:** Catch them doing the right thing, a lot. Children who get plenty of
attention for good behavior have less reason to test for the other kind.

## Moment 11: "But why?"

*The little lawyer*

**What's going on:** Some whys are real curiosity. But a "why" right after an ask is often a
way to delay it or start a debate.

**Say this:**
[Give one short reason, once.]
**"Teeth now, because the sugar bugs come out at night."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"I told you why. Teeth now, please. Big brush or little brush?"**
- *Rung 2:* **"Great question. Ask me again after your teeth are brushed, and I'll tell you
  more."** [Walk with them to the bathroom.]

**Instead of:** A long explanation, then a debate about the explanation.
**Try:** One reason, then the ask again. Save the long talks for calm times.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Skip the reason. **"Teeth time,"** and help.
- **4–5:** A short, concrete reason helps: **"so your teeth stay strong."**
- **6–7:** They may want to argue the reason. Don't. **"That's a good thing to talk about
  after."**

**Next time:** Explain your family's rules when everyone is calm. Child psychiatrists
recommend setting clear rules and explaining why they matter.[^S8] A child who already knows
the why has less need to ask it in the moment.

---

## Moment 12: Breaking a rule you just set

*"I said no ball in the house."*

**What's going on:** Young children's memory for rules is short, especially when the ball
is right there. Sometimes it's forgetting. Sometimes it's testing. You don't need to work
out which. The response is the same.

**Say this:**
**"Rule check: balls are for outside. Do you want to take it outside or put it in the
basket?"**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 3:* **"The ball is going on the shelf until after lunch."** [Put it away calmly.]
- *Rung 4:* After lunch, give it back, and go outside together if you can.

**Instead of:** "What did I JUST say?"
**Try:** **"Rule check."** Two words that remind without blaming.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Move the temptation. Toddlers can't resist a ball in the living room. Put it
  where they can't reach it.
- **4–5:** Let them remind you of the rule: **"What's the ball rule?"**
- **6–7:** Make the rule together: **"Where should balls go so nothing breaks?"**

**Next time:** Fewer rules, said the same way every time. Three to five clear family rules
work better than twenty. Chapter 11 shows you how to write yours.

---

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Choose one battle to drop and one to hold.** Look at the struggles you have most often.
Pick one that doesn't really matter (mismatched socks, the blue cup) and let it go this
week. Then pick one that does matter, and hold it every time with the same calm words. You
can't win every battle, and you don't need to. Pick the ones worth following through on.
