# Chapter 1: One Clear Ask: Five Steps That End the Repeat Loop

Theo is four. He's on the living room floor, driving a toy truck through a city made of
couch cushions. His mom is in the kitchen, draining pasta.

"Theo, can you wash your hands for dinner?"

Nothing.

"Theo. Hands. Dinner's ready."

The truck keeps driving.

"Theo, I'm not going to keep asking you!"

She does keep asking. By the fourth time she's shouting from the kitchen doorway, the
pasta is going cold, and Theo finally drags himself to the sink, looking hurt.

Rewind the scene. This time, Theo's mom puts the pot down and walks over to the cushion
city. She crouches beside him. "Theo." He looks up. She smiles at the truck. "Big
delivery today. Hands in the sink, please. Dinner's ready." Then she waits, quietly,
looking at him.

Theo parks the truck. He goes to the sink.

Same boy. Same truck. Same dinner. The difference was in *how* she asked, and it took her
about ten seconds.

## The One Clear Ask

The **One Clear Ask** is five small steps that turn your first ask into the one that
counts. Each step fixes one of the reasons our words get lost. Child-development guides
from the CDC and the Child Mind Institute give the same core advice: get close, make it
clear and specific, give one direction at a time, and follow through.[^S2] [^S3] This
method puts that advice in an order you can remember while the pasta is boiling over.

Here are the five steps.

**1. Get close.**
**2. Get their eyes.**
**3. Say it short.**
**4. Wait.**
**5. Follow through.**

Let's take them one at a time.

## Step 1: Get Close

Walk over. Get within arm's reach if you can.

This is the step parents most want to skip, because it's easier to call from the kitchen.
But a request shouted from another room is easy to miss for a busy young child. Being
close tells your child, without a word, *this one matters.*

If you're worried this will take too long, compare it with the alternative. Walking over
takes about ten seconds. The loop of four or five shouted asks can take five minutes, and
it usually ends with both of you upset.

*Tip:* crouch down or sit next to them when you can. Being at their level is calmer than
standing over them, and it's easier to do step 2 from there.

## Step 2: Get Their Eyes

Before you ask, make sure your child is with you. Say their name and wait for them to look
up. If they don't, a light touch on the shoulder or a hand near what they're playing with
usually helps.

**"Theo."** [Wait for the look.]

You can join their world for a moment first. One sentence about what they're doing shows
that you noticed it, and it makes the switch to your request a little gentler:

**"Big delivery today."**
**"Wow, that tower is tall."**
**"You're really drawing a lot of dogs."**

Keep it to one sentence. You're not starting a long chat. You're just opening the door.

## Step 3: Say It Short

Now make the ask. Keep it short, clear and specific. Three rules help.

**Tell, don't ask.** "Can you put your shoes on?" sounds polite, but it's a question, and
"no" is a perfectly good answer to a question. Make it a kind statement instead:[^S3]

**"Shoes on, please."**

**Say what to do, not what to stop.** "Stop jumping!" tells your child what *not* to do,
but not what to do instead.[^S2] Name the behavior you want:

**"Feet on the floor, please."**

**One thing at a time.** "Go upstairs, brush your teeth, put your pajamas on and pick a
book" is four instructions. Young children have short attention spans, and one direction
at a time is much easier to follow.[^S2]

**"Teeth first, please."**

A good test: if your ask is more than ten words long, cut it. "Please" is welcome. So is a
warm voice. A lecture is not.

## Step 4: Wait

After you ask, stop talking and wait. Count to five slowly in your head.

This is harder than it sounds. Most of us start repeating ourselves after a second or
two. But a young brain needs a few seconds to stop one thing and start another, and
repeating the ask in that gap just adds more words to sort through.[^S3]

Stay close. Keep looking at them in a calm, friendly way. Silence plus your presence says
*I'm waiting, and I'm not going anywhere.*

Very often, somewhere around four or five, your child starts to move.

## Step 5: Follow Through

This is the step that makes all the others work.

**If they do it, notice it.** Say exactly what they did, right away.

**"You put your truck down and came to wash. Thank you."**
**"Shoes on, all by yourself."**

Specific words like these tell your child exactly what earned your attention, which
makes it more likely to happen again.[^S7]

**If they don't do it, don't start the loop again.** Instead of asking a second, third and
fourth time from further and further away, you take the next calm step yourself. In short:

- **Give one reminder, with a choice.** "Hands in the sink. Do you want to walk or hop?"
- **If they still don't, help them do it.** "I'll help you. Let's go together." Then gently
  take their hand and walk with them.

That's it. No threats, no countdowns, no "How many times do I have to tell you?" You
asked once, you reminded once, and then you made it happen, kindly.

Chapter 3 turns this into a full plan called the **Follow-Through Ladder**, including what
to do when helping doesn't work. For today, one reminder and a helping hand are enough.

### The One Clear Ask in action

*Coming in from the yard:*
[Walk over. Crouch.] **"Ruby."** [Wait for the look.] **"Great digging. Time to come
inside, please."** [Count to five.] **"You came right away. Thank you."**

*Turning off the bath tap:*
[Stand next to the tub.] **"Milo, look at me."** [Wait.] **"Bubbles are done. Arms up for
the towel, please."** [Count to five.] **"Arms up. Great."**

*Stopping the couch-jumping:*
[Walk over.] **"Kai."** [Wait.] **"Feet on the floor, please."** [Count to five. No
movement.] **"Feet on the floor. You can climb down, or I can help you."** [Still jumping.]
**"I'll help you."** [Lift him down calmly.]

## Five swaps to try this week

Small changes in wording make a big difference. Try these.

*Instead of:* "Can you put your shoes on?"
*Try:* **"Shoes on, please."**

*Instead of:* "Stop running!"
*Try:* **"Walk next to me, please."**

*Instead of:* "Clean up your room."
*Try:* **"Blocks in the bin, please."**

*Instead of:* "Bath time, okay?"
*Try:* **"Bath time."** (Drop the "okay?" It turns the ask back into a question.)

*Instead of:* "How many times do I have to tell you?"
*Try:* [Walk over.] **"Coat on, please. I'll wait."**

## Why your child might push back at first

The first few times you use the One Clear Ask, your child may seem surprised. Some
children test it: they ignore the first ask to see whether you'll really follow through.
That's normal. They're checking whether the new rule is real.

Stay calm and keep going. Every time you follow through, the first ask gets a little
more believable. That's how the loop breaks: not in one day, but one kept promise at a
time.

## Your 5-Minute Action

1. **Pick one request you repeat every day.** Shoes, hands, coming to the table. Just one.
2. **Use all five steps for that one request, every time, for three days.** Get close, get
   their eyes, say it short, wait, follow through.
3. **Notice how many times you had to ask.** Don't judge it. Just notice. You'll compare
   it later.

That's all. You now have a better first ask.

**POCKET CARD: THE ONE CLEAR ASK**

1. **Get close.** Walk over. Crouch down.
2. **Get their eyes.** Say their name. Wait for the look.
3. **Say it short.** Tell, don't ask. What to do. One thing.
4. **Wait.** Count to five in your head. Don't repeat.
5. **Follow through.** Notice it, or remind once with a choice, then help.
