# Chapter 6: Toys Away, Hands Helping: Cleanup and Chores

Clara is six. Getting dressed for school should take five minutes. This morning, it has
taken twenty. One sock is on. The other is somewhere under the bed. Clara is lying on the
floor, telling a long story to her stuffed rabbit.

Her mom has said "Get dressed!" more times than she can count. Every time, Clara says
"I am!" and keeps not getting dressed.

Cleanup, chores and getting ready all have the same problem: to a young child, they're
boring, they're slow, and they feel enormous. "Clean your room" sounds like one task to us.
To a four-year-old, it's a mountain with no path up it. The moments in this chapter work
by making the mountain smaller: one step at a time, often side by side with you.

---

## Moment 13: Toy cleanup

*"Clean up this mess!"*

**What's going on:** "Clean up" is too big and too vague for a young child. They don't
know where to start, so they don't.

**Say this:**
[Get close. Give one specific step.]
**"Cars in the red bin, please."**
[When the cars are in:] **"Cars done! Now books on the shelf."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Cars in the bin. Do you want to beat the timer or beat the song?"**
- *Rung 2:* **"You do the cars, I'll do the blocks."** [Start cleaning beside them.]
- *Rung 3:* **"The toys still on the floor are going in the Sunday box until tomorrow."**

**Instead of:** "Clean up your room!" (from the doorway)
**Try:** **"Blocks in the bin. Then books on the shelf. Then we're done."**

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** One kind of toy at a time, with you. Sing a cleanup song while you do it.
- **4–5:** Make it a race against a song or a timer. Lots of four-year-olds love beating a
  song.
- **6–7:** A picture checklist on the door: bed, floor, books. They tick it off themselves.

**Next time:** Fewer toys out at once, and clean up before the next activity starts.
**"When the trains are away, then we'll get the paints out."**

---

## Moment 14: "I can't do it!"

*When they can*

**What's going on:** Your child may be tired, the task may feel too big, or they may just
want some of your help and closeness. It isn't a trick. It's usually a request for
connection dressed up as helplessness.

**Say this:**
**"You can do the first part. You put your foot in, and I'll help with the strap."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 2:* **"Let's do it together this time."** [Do it side by side, calmly.]
- *Rung 4:* **"All done. Tomorrow you can show me how you do it by yourself."**

**Instead of:** "You're a big kid, you can do it yourself!"
**Try:** **"I'll start it, you finish it."**

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** They often really can't yet. Do it together and name each step as you go.
- **4–5:** Break it down: **"First the arm. Now the other arm. Now the zip."**
- **6–7:** Offer help with one part only: **"Which part do you want help with?"**

**Next time:** Practice new skills when nobody is in a hurry, like doing up a coat on a
Saturday, not at 7:50 on a school morning. A skill practiced when everyone is calm is easier
to use under pressure.

---

## Moment 15: The 20-minute dawdle

*Getting dressed, putting shoes on, finishing anything*

**What's going on:** Getting dressed is several tasks pretending to be one. Every pause
between steps is a chance to wander off into a game. Your child isn't slow on purpose. The
steps just aren't holding their attention.

**Say this:**
[Stay close. One step at a time.]
**"Socks."** [Wait.] **"Now shirt."** [Wait.] **"Now pants."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Shirt next. Do you want to race me or race the timer?"**
- *Rung 2:* **"When you're dressed, then you can play until we leave."** Whatever time is left
  after getting dressed is playtime, so dawdling uses up their own play.
- *Rung 4:* No complaining in the car about how long it took. **"You got dressed. Let's go."**

**Instead of:** "Hurry up! Hurry up! We're going to be late!"
**Try:** **"Shirt next. I'll watch."** Your calm presence keeps them on track better than
"hurry."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Dress them, and let them do one part: **"You pull the sock up."**
- **4–5:** A picture chart of the steps, so they can see what's next without you saying it.
- **6–7:** Lay clothes out the night before and let them dress themselves while you're
  nearby.

**Next time:** Get clothes ready the night before, and get dressed before anything fun,
like breakfast or a show. *First* dressed, *then* fun.

## Moment 16: Bath time

*Getting in, and getting out*

**What's going on:** Getting into the bath means stopping play. Getting out means stopping
a different kind of play. Both are transitions, and transitions are hard.

**Safety first:** stay with your child the whole time they're in the bath.

**Say this (getting in):**
**"Bath time. Do you want to bring the ducks or the boat?"**

**Say this (getting out):**
**"Two more splashes, then the plug comes out."**
[After two splashes:] **"Plug out. Arms up for the towel."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 2:* **"Your hands or my hands?"** [Lift them out calmly and wrap them in the towel.]
- *Rung 3 (for splashing water out of the tub):* **"Water stays in the tub. If it splashes out
  again, bath time is over."** If it does, calmly pull the plug and lift them out.

**Instead of:** "Get in the bath NOW!" or "Get out, the water's freezing, I'm not telling you
again!"
**Try:** **"Plug out in two splashes."** Let the plug do the talking.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Let them pull the plug and wave bye-bye to the water.
- **4–5:** Make getting out a game: **"Can you get out like a penguin?"**
- **6–7:** Let them set the timer: **"Ten minutes or fifteen?"** Then the timer is the boss.

**Next time:** Keep the same order every night: bath, pajamas, teeth, story. When the next
step is always the same, it's easier to move toward it.

---

## Moment 17: Helping around the house

*Setting the table, laundry, feeding the pet*

**What's going on:** Many young children love to help, just not at the moment you ask, and
not with the job you picked. Real jobs can help children feel that they belong and that
they matter in the family.

**Say this:**
**"Table captain! Can you put a spoon by every plate, please?"**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Spoons or napkins? You pick."**
- *Rung 2:* **"Let's do it together. You carry, I'll set."**
- *Rung 4:* **"You set the spoons. Dinner looks great because of you."**

**Instead of:** "You never help around here!"
**Try:** Give the job a name and a title. "Table captain," "sock matcher," "fish feeder."

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Tiny jobs with you: carry the napkins, put socks in the basket.
- **4–5:** One job of their own, the same one every day.
- **6–7:** Two or three jobs on a chart they can check off themselves.

**Next time:** Make the job part of a routine, not a request. When "table captain" happens
every dinner, you'll find yourself asking less and less.

---

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Give your child one real job and a name for it.** Pick something small they can do every
day. Tell them their title, show them how to do it once, and thank them specifically each
time they do it.

**PRINTABLE: OUR "WHEN… THEN…" CHART**

*Put it on the fridge. Point to it instead of repeating yourself.*

**When** I'm dressed, **then** I can play until we leave.
**When** my toys are away, **then** we get out something new.
**When** my teeth are brushed, **then** we read a story.
**When** my pajamas are on, **then** I pick the book.
**When** ___________________, **then** ___________________.
**When** ___________________, **then** ___________________.

*Tip: the "then" should be something that was going to happen anyway. You're showing the
order of the day, not offering a new prize.*
