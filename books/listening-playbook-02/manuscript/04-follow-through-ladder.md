# Chapter 3: What to Do When They Still Don't: The Follow-Through Ladder

Eli is five. There are building bricks across the whole living room floor, and dinner is
ready in ten minutes.

His mom has read Chapter 1. She walks over, crouches down, and waits until Eli looks up.
"That's a huge spaceship. Bricks in the bin, please. Dinner's almost ready." Then she waits
and counts to five in her head.

Eli looks at her. Then he looks back at his spaceship and keeps building.

This is the moment where most parents slide back into the old loop. You asked nicely. You
did everything right. And your child still didn't do it. The next thing out of your mouth
is usually a repeat, then a louder repeat, then a threat.

This chapter gives you something better: a calm, step-by-step plan for the moment after
"no." It's called the **Follow-Through Ladder**, and once you know it, you'll never again
have to wonder what comes next.

## The Follow-Through Ladder

The ladder has four rungs. You start at the bottom and climb only as far as you need to.
Most of the time, you'll stop at the first or second rung.

**Rung 1: One reminder, with a choice.**
**Rung 2: Helping hands, or "When… then…"**
**Rung 3: A calm consequence.**
**Rung 4: Move on.**

The ladder works for two reasons. First, every rung is calm. Nothing on it requires a
raised voice. Second, it's predictable. Your child soon learns that once you've asked,
the ask will happen, one way or another. That predictability is what makes the first ask
start to count.

## Rung 1: One Reminder, With a Choice

If your child hasn't started after you've waited, give **one** reminder. This is the only
repeat on the whole ladder.

Make the reminder a little different from the first ask by adding a choice. The choice is
never about *whether* the task happens. It's about *how*.

**"Bricks in the bin. Do you want to start with the red ones or the blue ones?"**
**"Shoes on. Do you want to sit on the step or on the floor?"**
**"Time for your bath. Do you want to hop there like a frog or walk like a robot?"**

Choices work because they give your child a small piece of control in a moment where they
feel they have none. A child choosing between red and blue bricks has stopped arguing
about whether to clean up.

*Two rules for good choices:* keep it to two, and only offer options you're happy with
either way.

**If a consequence might follow, Rung 1 is where you say so.** Give one calm warning, so
your child knows exactly what will happen.[^S2] Keep it a fact, not a threat:

**"Bricks in the bin, please. Any bricks still on the floor will go on the high shelf
until tomorrow."**

Then wait again. Count to five.

## Rung 2: Helping Hands, or "When… Then…"

If your child still hasn't started, you don't ask a third time. You help it happen.

**Helping hands** works for anything you can physically help with, and it's especially
good for toddlers and preschoolers.

**"I'll help you get started. You put one in, I'll put one in."**
**"Your hands or my hands?"** [Then gently guide their hands, or do it together.]
**"Let's go together."** [Take their hand and walk to the bathroom.]

Helping isn't giving in. You're not doing the task *for* them. You're doing it *with*
them, so it gets done and they're part of it. For many young children, the hard part
was getting started. Once your hands are in the bin, theirs often follow.

**"When… then…"** works for things you can't physically help with, or for older children
who don't want hands-on help. It links the task to what naturally comes next.

**"When the bricks are in the bin, then we'll go outside."**
**"When your pajamas are on, then we'll read the story."**
**"When your coat is on, then we'll go to the park."**

Notice the word *when*, not *if*. "*If* you put the bricks away…" makes it sound optional.
"*When*" says it's going to happen; the only question is how soon.

Also notice what comes after "then." It's something that was going to happen anyway:
going outside, the story, the park. You're not inventing a new prize. You're just making the
order clear.

## Rung 3: A Calm Consequence

If your child still won't, it's time for a consequence. This is the rung parents worry
about most, so here's what a consequence is, and what it isn't.

A good consequence is **related, small and soon**. The American Academy of Child and
Adolescent Psychiatry advises that,
whenever possible, consequences should come right away, relate to the rule that was
broken, and be short enough that you can move on quickly.[^S8]

**Related.** It's connected to what happened. Bricks left on the floor go on the high shelf.
Water splashed out of the tub means bath time ends. The lesson makes sense to your child.

**Small.** It's short. The rest of the afternoon, not the rest of the month. A small
consequence you'll actually do teaches far more than a huge one you won't.

**Soon.** It happens now, not on Saturday. For a young child, a consequence that comes
days later isn't connected to anything.

And say it calmly, with no extra words:

**"You didn't put the bricks away, so the bricks are going on the high shelf until
tomorrow."**

Then do it. Pick up the bricks, without anger, and put them on the shelf.

Your child may cry or protest. That's fine. You don't need to argue, explain again or
take it back. You can say, **"You're disappointed. You can try again tomorrow."** The
consequence does the teaching. Your calm keeps it from turning into a fight.

### What a consequence is not

Consequences are not punishments meant to make a child suffer. They're not yelling,
shaming, name-calling or hitting. The American Academy of Pediatrics concluded that harsh
approaches like these don't work in the long term,[^S4] and child psychiatrists advise
against yelling and name-calling too.[^S8]

A good consequence also never takes away something your child *needs*: food, sleep,
comfort, or their time with you.

### Threats you won't keep

"If you don't clean up right now, we're never going to the park again!"
"No birthday party!"
"I'm throwing all your toys in the trash!"

We've all said things like this. They come out when we're out of ideas. But they're big,
far away and almost impossible to carry out, and children learn that quickly. Each empty
threat teaches your child that your words don't always mean what they say, which is
exactly the opposite of what we want.

If you hear a threat coming, stop and swap it for a small, related consequence you know
you'll follow through on.

## Rung 4: Move On

This is the rung most parents forget, and it matters more than it seems.

Once the task is done (or the consequence has happened), **it's over.** No lecture. No "I
told you so." No bringing it up again at dinner. No cold shoulder for the rest of the
evening.

**"Okay. Dinner time. Come sit with me."**

Moving on shows your child that the ask was about the bricks, not about whether you love
them. It also gives the next ask a fresh start. A child who's still in trouble from the
last ask has very little reason to cooperate with the next one.

Later, when things are calm, you can catch the good moments. That's next.

## Catch Them Listening

The ladder handles the moments when your child doesn't listen. But the fastest way to get
more listening is to notice the times they *do*.

Psychologist Alan Kazdin calls this looking for the **positive opposite**: decide what
you want your child to do, then notice and praise it when it happens.[^S7] The praise works
best when it's specific and comes right away. "I saw that you put your toys away!" tells
your child exactly what to do again. "Good job!" doesn't.[^S9]

**"You came the first time I asked. Thank you."**
**"You turned off the tablet when the timer rang. That was hard, and you did it."**
**"You put your shoes on while I got your coat. That helped us get out the door."**

Aim to notice more listening than you correct. It feels strange at first, especially on a
hard day. But children work hard for attention, and this is the kind of attention you
want them working for.

## Rewards and Bribes: What's the Difference?

Parents often ask whether rewards are just bribes with a nicer name. Here's how this book
draws the line.

**A reward is agreed before, when everyone is calm.**
**"When you're dressed before the timer rings, you get to pick the song in the car."**

**A bribe is offered in the middle of a standoff, to make it stop.**
"Okay, okay, if you put your shoes on *right now*, you can have a cookie."

The reward teaches: *doing the task gets something good.* The bribe teaches: *refusing
long enough makes the offers get better.* Same cookie, very different lesson.

Rewards work best when they come right after the behavior,[^S9] and the best ones are
often small: picking the song, an extra story, five minutes of your full attention. Keep
the rewards for the hardest tasks, and let your specific praise do the rest of the work.

## Two Times to Skip the Ladder

**When safety is at stake, skip straight to action.** If your child is running toward the
street, reaching for the stove, or about to hurt someone, don't count to five. Move,
block, pick them up, hold their hand. Talk about it afterward, once everyone is safe.

**When "won't" turns into a meltdown, switch to feelings first.** Sometimes a child on the
ladder stops refusing and starts falling apart: crying, screaming, kicking, overwhelmed. A
child in a meltdown can't follow instructions. Pause the ladder, help them calm down, and
come back to the task once they're calm. The task still happens; it just happens later.

If you have *The Meltdown Playbook*, this is where its 3-Line Meltdown Script comes in:
**"I see you're ___. I'm right here. I won't let you ___."** If you don't, a calm voice,
few words and staying close will carry you through.

## The Ladder in Action

Let's go back to Eli and his spaceship.

His mom has asked once and waited. Eli kept building. So she moves to **Rung 1**:

**"Bricks in the bin. Do you want to start with the wings or the engine?"**

Eli says, "No! I'm not done!"

She stays calm and adds the warning: **"Bricks on the floor will go on the high shelf
until tomorrow."** Then she waits.

Eli doesn't move. So she climbs to **Rung 2**, helping hands:

**"I'll help you start. I'll take off one wing, you take off the other."**

She takes a wing apart and drops the pieces in the bin. Eli watches. Then, grumbling, he
pulls off the other wing. They finish together in two minutes.

**Rung 4:** **"Done. Thanks for finishing. Let's go eat."** No lecture about how it should
have happened the first time.

Eli's mom never needed Rung 3 that night. That's how it goes most of the time. The ladder
isn't about consequences. It's about always knowing your next calm step, so you never need
the yell.

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Fill in your Consequence Picker.** Choose the three moments at home where you most often
end up repeating yourself or yelling. For each one, write down:

1. **A related, small, soon consequence** you'd be willing to carry out every time.
2. **The exact words** you'll use to give the warning at Rung 1.

Do this now, while you're calm. In the moment, you won't have to think. You'll just read
the words you already chose.

**POCKET CARD: THE FOLLOW-THROUGH LADDER**

1. **One reminder, with a choice.** Add the warning if a consequence is coming.
2. **Helping hands, or "When… then…"** Do it with them, or link it to what's next.
3. **A calm consequence.** Related. Small. Soon. Said once.
4. **Move on.** No lecture. No grudge. Fresh start.

*Safety first: skip the ladder and act. Meltdown: feelings first, task later.*
