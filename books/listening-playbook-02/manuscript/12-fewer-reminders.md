# Part 3: A Home That Listens

*The best ask is the one you don't need to make.*

# Chapter 11: Fewer Reminders: Let the Routine Do the Talking

It's 7:30 on a school morning. Ada is six and her brother Sol is three. In the last half
hour, their dad has said: "Get up." "Get dressed." "Not that shirt." "Eat your toast." "Sit
down." "Stop kicking your brother." "Teeth." "Shoes." "Where's your backpack?" "Coat." "Hurry
up." "COAT."

By the time they reach the car, he's given more instructions than he can count, and
everyone is cross.

Parts 1 and 2 of this book help you make each ask count. This last part is about needing
fewer of them. When the day has a shape that everyone knows, the routine does a lot of the
talking for you, and your words matter more because there are fewer of them.

## Sort your asks into three piles

Look back at the tally you kept in Chapter 2. Then sort your everyday asks into three
piles:

**Must.** Safety, health and getting out the door. Car seats, hand-holding near roads,
teeth, coats in the cold. These you follow through on every time.

**Matters.** A few things you care about for your family: kind words, toys away before
dinner, sitting for meals. Pick no more than three or four.

**Let go.** Everything else. The mismatched socks, the shirt you wouldn't have picked, the
way they eat their toast. Let these go, at least for now.

Every ask you drop from the third pile makes the first two piles easier to follow through
on. It's much easier to mean every ask when there are fewer of them.

## Write your family rules

A few clear family rules can replace dozens of separate instructions. Child psychiatrists
recommend setting clear rules and explaining why they matter.[^S8] Here's how to make them
work for young children.

**Keep them few.** Three to five rules. More than that, and nobody remembers them.

**Say what to do.** "We use gentle hands" works better than "No hitting." Just like the One
Clear Ask, a rule should tell your child what *to* do.

**Keep them short.** Each rule should fit on one line and be easy to say out loud.

**Make them together.** Ask your child: **"What rules would help our family get along?"**
Rules your child helped write feel like theirs, not just yours, and even a three-year-old
can choose the picture that goes next to a rule.

**Explain the why, once.** "We walk inside, so nobody gets hurt." Then post the rules where
everyone can see them.

Some examples to get you started:

- **We use gentle hands.**
- **We use kind words.**
- **We walk inside.**
- **We put toys away before new ones come out.**
- **When a grown-up asks, we answer.**

Once your rules are up, you can use the short reminder from Moment 12: **"Rule check."**
Point to the poster. Let the rule do the work, instead of starting a new speech.

## Let the chart be the boss

For the parts of the day that happen the same way every time (mornings, bedtime, getting
out the door), a **picture routine chart** can save you dozens of instructions.

Draw or print a simple picture for each step, in order: toilet, clothes, breakfast, teeth,
shoes, coat. Put it where it's needed, at your child's eye level.

Then, instead of giving the instruction, ask about the chart:

**"What's next on the chart?"**

This small change does a lot. Your child finds the answer themselves, so it doesn't feel
like being bossed. You don't have to repeat yourself, because the chart says it for you.
And when they're stuck, the chart is the one asking, not you.

If you have *The Meltdown Playbook*, its routine chart in Chapter 7 works well here.

## Give warnings that work

Many listening battles start with a surprise. One minute your child is playing; the next,
you need them in the car. A warning before the switch makes the ask easier to follow.

Make the warning **concrete**. "Five more minutes" means little to a young child. **"Two more
pages, then bath"** or **"One more turn on the slide, then shoes"** is something they can count.

Then, when the warning runs out, use the One Clear Ask, and follow through.

## Turn chores into jobs

A child with a job doesn't need as many instructions. Moment 17 showed you how to give
your child a named job, like "table captain" or "sock matcher." Once a job is part of the
routine, it stops being an ask at all. It's just what happens.

## Fill their cup before you ask

Some asks go badly simply because your child hasn't had much of your attention that
day. A few minutes of one-on-one time, where your child leads the play and you just join
in, can make the rest of the day go more smoothly. In *The Meltdown Playbook*, this is
called "special time." Even five minutes a day helps.

It sounds like the opposite of discipline. But a child who feels connected to you has more
reason to cooperate when you ask.

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Write your family rules together and post them.** Sit down with your child this week.
Choose three to five rules, word them as what to *do*, and add a drawing or picture for
each one. Put the poster where everyone will see it, and start using "Rule check."
