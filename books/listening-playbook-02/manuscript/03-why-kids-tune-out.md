# Chapter 2: Why Kids Tune Out (and Why Yelling Seems to Work)

Isla is three. She's building a train track across the hallway, piece by careful piece.
From the bathroom, her dad calls, "Isla, bath time!"

Isla keeps building.

"Isla! Bath!"

She clicks another piece into place.

Her dad sighs. *She heard me. She's just ignoring me.* He calls again, louder, and feels
the familiar mix of tired and annoyed creeping in.

But here's the thing. Isla may have heard the sound of his voice. That doesn't mean the
words made it all the way in, or that she could switch off the train in the time he gave
her. To a grown-up, "bath time" is a simple request. To a three-year-old in the middle of
building something, it's a much bigger ask than it sounds.

This chapter explains what's going on, so that the next time your child seems to ignore
you, you know what to fix. It also explains something that confuses almost every parent:
why yelling seems to be the only thing that works.

## Stopping is hard work for a young brain

Think about what you're really asking when you say "bath time" to a child who's playing.
You're asking them to:

- notice your voice over the game in their head,
- stop doing something they love,
- remember what "bath time" means right now,
- and start doing something else.

Each of those steps uses brain skills that grown-ups take for granted: shifting attention,
holding a plan in mind, and stopping an action you want to keep doing. Researchers group
these skills under the name **executive function**. Children aren't born with them. They
are born with the ability to build them, and these skills keep developing through the
whole of childhood and into early adulthood.[^S1]

So when a three-year-old doesn't stop playing the moment you ask, it's not a sign of
disrespect. It's a sign of being three.

### Most of us expect too much, too early

You're not alone if this surprises you. In a national survey by ZERO TO THREE, 56% of
parents said children can resist doing something forbidden before age 3, and more than a
third said children under 2 can do it. The same report notes that brain research shows this kind of self-control
only *starts* to develop between about three and a half and four years.[^S6]

That gap between what we expect and what our children can do is behind a lot of daily
frustration. When we expect instant listening from a three-year-old, every slow response
feels personal. When we know that stopping is hard for them, we can make it easier, and
we stay calmer while we do.

This doesn't mean young children can't learn to listen. They can, and they do. It just
means they need help to do it: a clear ask, a little time, and a grown-up who follows
through.

## Five ways our words get lost

Even when a child *can* listen, the way we ask can make it much harder. Here are the five
most common ways a request gets lost on the way, and the step of the One Clear Ask that
fixes each one.

**1. We ask from far away.** A voice from another room is easy to tune out, especially
during play. *Fix: get close.*[^S3]

**2. We ask while they're focused on something else.** Their eyes are on the train, so
their attention is too. *Fix: get their eyes first.*[^S2]

**3. We ask a question when we mean an instruction.** "Do you want to have your bath now?"
invites "no." *Fix: tell, don't ask.*[^S3]

**4. We use too many words.** A request buried in a speech, or four instructions in a row,
is hard for a young child to sort out. *Fix: say it short, one thing at a time.*[^S2]

**5. Nothing happens after the first ask.** This is the big one, and it deserves its own
section.

## The real reason yelling seems to work

Let's go back to Isla.

Her dad asks once from the bathroom. Nothing happens. He asks again. Nothing happens. He
asks a third time, louder. Still nothing. Finally, on the fourth ask, he marches into the
hallway, raises his voice, and stands over the train track. *Now* Isla gets up.

What did Isla learn from that evening? She learned that the first three asks didn't
need an answer. Nothing happened after them. The ask that counted was the one where her
dad's voice changed and he walked over.

And what did her dad learn? He learned that yelling works. Isla only moved when he
yelled.

But look again. On that fourth ask, two things happened at the same time:

1. He raised his voice.
2. He walked over and made it clear that the bath was happening now.

It's very easy to give the credit to number 1. In fact, number 2 did the work. The yell
just happened to arrive at the same moment as the follow-through.

**Children don't learn to listen to yelling. They learn to listen to follow-through.**

When the follow-through only comes with a yell, they learn to wait for the yell. When the
follow-through comes calmly, on the first ask, they learn to listen to the first ask.

So the fix isn't to stop caring, or to never raise your voice again. It's simpler than
that:

**Keep the follow-through. Drop the yell.**

### Yelling costs more than it gives

There's another reason to move the follow-through earlier. Yelling doesn't work as well as
it seems to.

The American Academy of Pediatrics reviewed the research on discipline and concluded that
harsh approaches, including yelling at or shaming children, do little to change behavior
in the short term and don't work in the long term.[^S4] A yell may get your child moving
tonight, but it doesn't teach them to listen tomorrow. And once the yell is the signal
that counts, your child has every reason to keep waiting for it.

If you read that and felt a pang of guilt, please hear this too: you are far from the
only one. In a large national study of American parents, almost all said they had yelled,
screamed or shouted at their child to correct behavior.[^S5] Yelling isn't a sign that
you're a bad parent. It's a sign that you're a parent without a better plan for the
moment after "no." This book is that plan.

## Connection before direction

There's one more reason children tune us out, and it's a gentle one. Often, our asks
arrive out of nowhere. We've been busy, they've been busy, and then suddenly we appear
with an instruction.

A few seconds of connection before the ask can make a big difference. That's why Step 2 of
the One Clear Ask includes joining their world for a moment:

**"That's a long track! Two more pieces, then bath."**
**"Your dragon looks fierce. Time to put him to sleep for dinner."**

Notice the second example. It doesn't just connect. It gives the child a way to finish
the game in their own head, which makes stopping easier.

If you've read *The Meltdown Playbook*, this will sound familiar. It's the same idea as
the first step of the Calm Words Formula: show your child that you see what they care
about before you ask for something. Children who feel seen are more willing to switch
gears.

You don't need to do this every single time. But for the hard transitions (leaving the
park, ending a game, bath time, bedtime), a moment of connection is time well spent.

## What to fix first

None of this means you've been doing it wrong. It means you now know which levers to pull.
The biggest one is the moment after you've asked clearly, waited, and your child still
doesn't move. That's where most families slide back into the repeat loop, and it's what
Chapter 3 is about.

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Count your asks for one hour.** Pick a busy hour (the morning rush, or the hour before
dinner) and keep a quick tally on your phone or a sticky note. Every instruction you give
your child gets a mark. Every *repeat* of an instruction gets a circle.

No judgment, no fixing. Just count.

Most parents are surprised by both numbers. If your marks are high, Chapter 11 will help
you cut the number of instructions you need to give. If your circles are high, Chapter 3
is about to help with that.
