# Chapter 8: Whining, Interrupting and Rude Words

Iris is six. Her mom is on the phone with the doctor's office, trying to book an
appointment. "Mom. Mom. Mom. MOM. Mooooom." Iris tugs her sleeve. Then she whines, "You're
not *listening* to me!"

Her mom covers the phone. "Not now!" Two seconds later: "Mom. Mom. Mom."

The moments in this chapter are less about doing a task and more about *how* your child
talks to you. Whining, interrupting, silly-rude words and back-talk are all ways of getting
your attention. The trick is to teach your child a better way to get it, and then make
sure the better way works.

---

## Moment 23: The whiny voice

*"But I waaaaant it!"*

**What's going on:** Whining is hard to ignore, which is exactly why it works. If the whine
gets a faster answer than the regular voice, your child will keep using it.

**Say this:**
**"I want to help. I can't understand the whiny voice. Can you ask me in your regular
voice?"**
[When they do, answer quickly, even if the answer is no.]

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Try again in your regular voice. Like this:"** [Show them, in a calm voice.]
- *Rung 2:* Turn your attention to something else until the regular voice comes back. The
  CDC suggests this for minor behaviors like whining, and warns that it may get worse
  when you first start.[^S11]
- *Rung 4:* The moment the regular voice comes back, turn to them warmly. **"Thanks for
  asking like that. What did you need?"**

**Instead of:** Giving in after the tenth whine.
**Try:** Answer the regular voice, every time. Ignore the whine, every time.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Give them the words to copy: **"Say, 'Milk, please.'"**
- **4–5:** Practice "whiny voice" and "regular voice" as a game when everyone's calm. It's
  often funny, and it makes the difference clear.
- **6–7:** A quiet signal works: touch your ear to mean "I'll listen to your regular voice."

**Next time:** Whining can show up when kids are hungry, tired or haven't had much of
you that day. A snack and a few minutes of real attention can prevent a lot of it.

---

## Moment 24: Interrupting

*When you're on the phone or talking to someone*

**What's going on:** Young children find waiting very hard, and they don't yet know how to
get your attention politely. To them, everything feels urgent right now.

**Say this:**
[Teach this when calm:] **"When I'm talking and you need me, put your hand on my arm. I'll
put my hand on yours, so you know I know you're waiting. Then I'll turn to you as soon as
I can."**

[In the moment:] **"I'm talking to Grandma. Hand on my arm, please."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Hand on my arm. I'll be with you in a minute."** [Then turn back to your call.]
- *Rung 2:* If the interrupting keeps going, gently turn your attention back to your call
  and keep it there. The CDC lists interrupting as one of the minor behaviors where
  redirecting your attention works best.[^S11]
- *Rung 4:* Turn to them as soon as you can, and thank them: **"You waited with your hand
  on my arm. Thank you. What did you want to tell me?"**

**Always say this too:** **"If someone is hurt or something's dangerous, interrupt me right
away."**

**Instead of:** "Not now! Not now! I said NOT NOW!"
**Try:** **"Hand on my arm."** Then keep your promise to turn to them soon.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** They can only wait a few seconds. Keep calls short, or keep a "phone basket" of
  special toys for call time.
- **4–5:** Keep the wait short at first, then slowly make it longer as they get better at it.
- **6–7:** Teach them to say **"Excuse me"** and wait for you to look at them.

**Next time:** Before a call, give your child a few minutes of attention and set them up
with something to do. **"I'm going to make a phone call. Here's your drawing pad. Hand on my
arm if you need me."**

## Moment 25: Potty words and silly-rude talk

*"Poopy-head!" (followed by giggles)*

**What's going on:** Many preschoolers discover that certain words get big reactions:
laughter from siblings, shock from adults. Big reactions make the words more fun.

**Say this:**
[Calm, almost bored:] **"Potty words are for the bathroom."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"If you want to say potty words, you can go to the bathroom and say them
  there."**
- *Rung 2:* Don't react. Turn the conversation to something else. Without an audience,
  the words lose their magic.
- *Rung 3 (if it's aimed at someone):* **"That word hurt your sister's feelings. Let's take
  a break from playing together for a few minutes."**

**Instead of:** Gasping, laughing, or a long talk about bad words.
**Try:** The same calm line, every time: **"Potty words are for the bathroom."**

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** They often don't know what the words mean. A calm "We don't say that" and a
  change of subject is enough.
- **4–5:** The bathroom rule works well at this age. It's funny, and it's fair.
- **6–7:** Talk, when calm, about words that hurt versus words that are just silly.

**Next time:** Give silly a time and place. A few minutes of "silly word time" at bath time
can take the fun out of saying them at the dinner table.

---

## Moment 26: "You're stupid!" "Go away!"

*Rude words when they're not really upset*

**What's going on:** Your child is trying out strong words to see what they do. If they're
upset and overwhelmed, that's a different moment: see *The Meltdown Playbook*, Moment 30.
This moment is the calm, testing version.

**Say this:**
**"That's a hurtful word. You can say, 'I don't like that.'"**
Or: **"Try that again in a kind way."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"I'll listen when you can say it kindly. Try again."**
- *Rung 2:* Step away briefly and calmly. **"I'm going to finish the dishes. I'll come back
  when you're ready to talk kindly."**
- *Rung 4:* When they try again, respond warmly. **"Thanks. Now I understand. You don't want
  to stop playing."**

**Instead of:** "Don't you dare talk to me like that!"
**Try:** **"Try that again in a kind way."** Calm, short, and gives them a way back.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Give them the words: **"Say, 'Stop, please.'"**
- **4–5:** Teach the difference between feeling words and hurting words: **"You can say 'I'm
  mad.' You can't call names."**
- **6–7:** When calm, ask what they were trying to say. Often it's "I'm angry and I don't know
  how to say it."

**Next time:** Practice the words for strong feelings ("I'm mad," "That's not fair," "I need
a break") when everyone is calm, so they're ready when your child needs them.

---

## Moment 27: Eye-rolls and back-talk

*Ages 5 to 7: "Ugh. Fine. Whatever."*

**What's going on:** Older preschoolers and early school-age kids are practicing
independence, and they sometimes copy tones they hear from older kids or on screens. An
eye-roll is usually a feeling, not a plan.

**Say this:**
[Let the eye-roll go. Respond to the task, not the attitude.]
**"Backpack on the hook, please."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* If the words are rude: **"I'll listen when you say it in a respectful voice. Try
  again."**
- *Rung 2:* Follow through on the original ask as usual. **"When your backpack is on the
  hook, then you can go play."**
- *Rung 3:* Save consequences for what they *do* (not hanging the backpack), not for an
  eye-roll.

**Instead of:** "Don't you roll your eyes at me!"
**Try:** Ignore the eye-roll. Follow through on the ask. Talk about tone later, when calm.

**Age tweaks:**

- **5:** Often copying. Name it lightly: **"Whoa, that was a big sigh. Want to try that
  again?"**
- **6–7:** When calm, talk about it: **"When you said 'whatever,' it sounded like you were
  really frustrated. What was going on?"**

**Next time:** Back-talk can show up when kids feel bossed around all day. More choices,
a few minutes of one-on-one time, and fewer instructions can take a lot of the heat out of
it.

---

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Teach the "hand on my arm" signal today.** Practice it twice when nothing is at stake:
pretend to be on the phone, let your child put their hand on your arm, cover it with yours,
and then turn to them with a smile. The first real phone call will go better for the
practice.
