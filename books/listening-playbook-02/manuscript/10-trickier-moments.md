# Chapter 9: Trickier Moments

Oscar is five. At the grocery store, he has squeezed every avocado in the bin, opened a box
of crackers "just to look," and is now standing on the bottom shelf of the cereal aisle.
His mom can feel other shoppers watching.

The three moments in this chapter happen away from home, or around a harder topic. Other
people may be watching, the rules may be different, and you may feel the pressure to fix
it *fast*. The same tools still work. They just work best when you plan ahead.

---

## Moment 28: Not listening at someone else's house

*Playdates and visits*

**What's going on:** New places are exciting, the rules are different, and your child may
be testing whether your rules still count when you're a guest. You may also feel judged,
which makes it harder to stay calm.

**Say this:**
[Before you go in:] **"At Nia's house, we follow Nia's mom's rules. Toys stay in the
playroom. When I say 'two more minutes,' it's almost time to go."**
[In the moment, crouched down, quietly:] **"At Nia's house, toys stay in the playroom,
please."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Toys in the playroom. Do you want to carry it back, or should I?"**
- *Rung 2:* Walk them back to the playroom yourself.
- *Rung 3:* **"It's hard to follow the rules today, so we're going home a little early. We'll
  try again next time."** Say goodbye politely and leave, without a scene or a lecture.

**Instead of:** Scolding in front of the other family.
**Try:** Crouch down and speak quietly, just to your child. Your ask is between the two of
you.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Stay close. Toddlers need you nearby in new places to follow any rule at all.
- **4–5:** Practice the rules in the car: **"What's the toy rule at Nia's house?"**
- **6–7:** Let them help plan: **"What will help you remember the rules there?"**

**Next time:** Plan your exit line before you arrive, and tell your child what it is.
**"When I say 'shoes on,' that means we're leaving in two minutes."** Then use the exact line
when it's time.

---

## Moment 29: Touching everything in a store

*Grabbing, squeezing, climbing the shelves*

**What's going on:** A store is full of bright, touchable things, and small hands want to
explore. Your child isn't being naughty. Their hands just don't have a job.

**Say this:**
**"You can look with your eyes. Hands on the cart, please."**
Or give them a job: **"Can you be the finder? Find me the bananas."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* **"Hands on the cart. Do you want to push with me or hold the list?"**
- *Rung 2:* **"I'll help your hands."** [Take their hand, or lift them into the cart seat.]
- *Rung 3:* **"We're leaving this aisle."** [Move on to the next aisle, calmly.]

**Instead of:** "Don't touch anything!"
**Try:** Give hands something to do: **"Hold the list," "Push the cart," "Put the apples in
the bag."**

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Use the cart seat. Give them one safe item to hold, like a box of pasta.
- **4–5:** "Eyes only" game: they spot things and tell you, but hands stay on the cart.
- **6–7:** Give them a short list of their own and a job: **"Find these three things."**

**Next time:** Before you go in, tell them their job. **"Today you're the finder. Your job is
the bananas and the yogurt."** A child with a job is less likely to make up their own.

## Moment 30: "I didn't do it!"

*Lying*

**What's going on:** You know there's crayon on the wall. Your child is holding the crayon.
And they're telling you they didn't do it.

Here's something that may help: lying is a normal step in development. Research led by
psychologist Kang Lee found that about 30% of verbal 2-year-olds try to fool their
parents at some point, about half of 3-year-olds do it regularly, and by ages 5 to 7 it's
seen in nearly all healthy children.[^S12] Lying takes thinking skills, like understanding
that you don't know what they know.

That doesn't make lying okay. But it does mean your child isn't on the way to becoming a
bad person. They're learning, and you can help them learn honesty.

**Say this:**
[Don't ask a question you already know the answer to. Say what you see.]
**"I see crayon on the wall. Let's clean it together."**

[If they tell you the truth:] **"Thank you for telling me the truth. That was brave. Now
let's fix it."**

**If they still don't:**
- *Rung 1:* If they insist they didn't do it, don't argue. **"The wall needs cleaning. Here's
  your cloth."**
- *Rung 3:* The consequence is about the *action*, not the lie: **"Crayons are put away for
  the afternoon."**
- *Rung 4:* Move on. No lecture about lying.

**Instead of:** "Did you draw on the wall?" (They know you know, and now they have to decide
whether to lie.) Or: "Don't you dare lie to me!"
**Try:** **"I see ___. What can we do to fix it?"**

Harsh punishment tends to backfire here. In one study, 3- and 4-year-olds from a school
with harsh punishments lied more often about breaking a rule than children from a school
without them.[^S13] Children who expect a big punishment have more reason to hide the truth.
Children who know they'll be helped to fix things have more reason to tell it.

**Age tweaks:**

- **2–3:** Often it's wishful thinking, not a plan to trick you. Just say what happened and
  help fix it.
- **4–5:** Praise honesty every time you see it: **"You told me the truth even though it was
  hard."**
- **6–7:** Talk, when calm, about trust: **"When you tell me the truth, I know I can believe
  you."**

**Next time:** Make telling the truth the easier choice. When your child admits something,
your first words should be thanks, then the fix. Over time, that's what they'll remember.

## Your 5-Minute Action

**Plan your exit line for the next visit.** Think of the next playdate, family visit or
store trip. Decide now on the one sentence that will mean "we're leaving soon," tell your
child before you go, and use it exactly when it's time.
